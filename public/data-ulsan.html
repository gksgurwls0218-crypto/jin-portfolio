Ulsan HD — the most chances in the league, and the latest

A team second on points has a goal difference of +4. That single number started this — twenty-six matches, opened one by one. The first two answers were wrong.

Han HyukjinSeptember 2026K League Data Portal12 min read

THE SHORT VERSION

  1. Ulsan lead K League 1 for key passes and shots on target. No club creates more.
  2. Those chances are concentrated after the break: second-half xG is 1.7× the first, whatever the half-time score.
  3. Yet they have never won from behind at half-time — 0 wins in 7. Strong after the break, but not a comeback side.
  4. They score at 0.95 of expected and concede at 1.09. The +4 goal difference is those two losses added together.
  5. The first two answers — “they win with less of the ball”, “fewer entries, more shots” — turned out to be artefacts of the scoreline.

00Why the table stopped me

This began with a single row

2nd — Ulsan HD · 26 matches · 12-4-10 · 40 points · 41 scored, 37 conceded · GD +4

Two things catch the eye. A second-placed side has lost ten matches. And its goal difference is +4. Seoul, first, are on +31; Jeonbuk, third, on +9 — a club below them with more than twice the margin. The points say second; the goals say fourth or fifth.

Usually that means one of two things: a side that keeps winning tight games, or one that loses badly when it loses. Ulsan are both. Several one-goal wins — and also two 1-4s, a 1-3 and a 2-3.

So the question set itself: how is this team second, and why is the goal difference only +4? What follows is the record of opening the data in order. The first two answers were wrong. I have left the wrong turns in. They were the most useful part.

01The first answer, and why it fails

Possession was measuring the scoreboard, not the style

Possession first. Ulsan average 43.6% in wins and 50.1% in defeats; the correlation between possession and points is −0.401. Season average 46.7%. Stop here and you get an answer: “Ulsan give the ball away and win on the counter.”

It is wrong. Change the window and it collapses.

The accurate statement is the reverse of the first one. Possession did not produce the results; the results produced the possession. Ahead, Ulsan drop off and concede the ball; behind, they hold it and chase. A season possession average is closer to a record of how often a side was in front than a description of how it plays.

02The second answer — half true at best

The same test, applied to “fewer entries, more shots”

Next, entries into the final third — completed passes that start behind the attacking third and end inside it. Ulsan make 26.6 per match; their opponents make 33.3. Yet Ulsan take 13.2 shots to their opponents’ 12.1. Fewer entries, more shots.

This is the first lesson in the data. Whenever an efficiency metric is computed over a whole season, ask one question: is this a number the scoreline produced? There is one way to check — recompute it over the goalless portion of each match.

03Where Ulsan sit among the twelve

Season totals, which the scoreline moves less

“Fewest long passes” is a count, not a proportion. Measured directly from the event feed, passes of 30m or more make up 12.0% of Ulsan’s passing and 11.8% of their opponents’. Mean pass length is 17.4m for both. They do not play a smaller share of long passes — they simply play fewer passes. “Ulsan don’t go long” is the wrong sentence; “Ulsan don’t pass much” is the right one.

04Shape of the attack — short and quick

Counted by possession spell

A spell ends when the opposition registers two consecutive events; that splits each match into possessions.

The last action before each shot tells the same story. 18% of Ulsan’s shots follow directly from a ball recovery (16% for opponents), while only 13% come from an open cross against 21%. Their entries into the final third also run more centrally — 25% through the middle against 18%.

This is not a side that crosses and contests. It wins the ball, moves it short and central, and finishes with a pass. Ulsan still swing in 19.8 crosses a match (24.9% find a team-mate), but crosses are not where their shots come from.

05A different team after the break

The one pattern that survives controlling for game state

148 shots and 15.98 xG before the break; 194 shots and 27.00 xG after. Ulsan create 1.7× as much in the second half. The obvious objection is that they were chasing games.

Passing moves the same way: 16.9m average and 10.5% of passes over 30m in the first half, rising to 18.0m and 13.9% in the second. They lengthen — trading accuracy for territory.

Why is beyond this data. For the record, the median substitution comes at 71 minutes and 56% of changes are made after the 70th. That is not evidence that substitutions cause it. Whether it is conditioning, a deliberate plan to hold something back, or opponents fading, only video and training information can say.

Being strong after the break is not the same as being a comeback side. Ulsan are the former. The second-half output mostly arrives while protecting or extending a lead; from behind, the chances came and the goals did not. Seven matches is a small sample — but zero wins is hard to wave away.

07Where the +4 comes from

Back to the opening question

If they create more than anyone, why is the goal difference only +4? xG attaches to each shot the number of goals it yields on average from that position and situation. Goals divided by xG equals 1.00 when a side scores exactly as expected.

Whether 1.45 reflects a defensive problem or simple variance cannot be settled here. Eleven goals against 7.61 xG is a 3.4-goal overshoot, and swings that size occur by chance inside 26 matches. The remaining rounds will answer it.

08The empty column: set pieces

What 110 corners produced

This reads as an unused asset rather than a weakness. The delivery is already there — 38.5% of their set-piece crosses find a team-mate against 32.1% for opponents — and the finishing inside the box is fine (0.96 goals per xG). What is missing is the link between them. At 0.04 goals per corner, this is the cheapest improvement available to them.

11Back to the table

The question was: how is this team second, and why is the goal difference only +4?

Second place is in sections 03 to 05. Ulsan do not circulate (eighth for passes) and reach the final pass in few touches (first for key passes). The ball they win becomes a shot within fifteen seconds 55.9% of the time, and half their shots end a spell of three passes or fewer. The result is the most shots on target in the league. Behind that, a defence that sits deep and takes the ball cleanly.

The +4 is in sections 06 and 07. That output is concentrated after the break, and from behind at half-time it does not convert into results (0 wins in 7). The chances they make return exactly what they are worth (0.95); the chances they concede return more (1.09), and at the edge of the box 1.45× more.

Ulsan create more than anyone in the league, and they create it later than anyone. Take a lead into half-time and they win seven of eight. Go in behind and no amount of second-half pressure has yet produced a win. Their ninety minutes are largely decided in the first forty-five.

And the first two answers in this piece were wrong. “They win with less of the ball” and “fewer entries, more shots” were both products of the scoreline. Recomputed over the goalless portion of each match, one reversed and the other mostly vanished. The first thing to doubt when reading data is not the data — it is the situation that produced it.

12Facing them, and their own to-do list

If you are preparing against Ulsan

  1. The first half is the match. Ulsan’s first-half xG runs at 0.61 per game, 60% of their second-half figure. Get in front before the break and they enter the 0-from-7 pattern.
  2. Manage the five seconds after you lose it. 55.9% of their shots come within fifteen seconds of a recovery and 47.4% within three passes. The danger is not their build-up; it is your turnover.
  3. The middle. A quarter of their final-third entries come centrally against 18% for the sides they face. Blocking the half-spaces matters more than blocking crosses.
  4. You can afford to give up corners. One goal from 110. No side in the league punishes them less.

Ulsan’s own list

  1. The empty first half. The second-half output is already close to the ceiling. The points are in raising that 0.61.
  2. 1.45× at the edge of the box. Variance or a real defensive problem — that needs separating.
  3. 110 corners, one goal. The delivery and the finishing both exist; the link does not.
  4. Reliance on Lee Dong-gyeong. One missed match is not enough to size it, but 23% of key passes and 27% of crosses is a risk on its own.

14A prediction instead of a conclusion

Four statements that can be wrong. The remaining twelve matches will mark them.

  1. The goal difference improves. If 0.95 and 1.09 are variance, both drift back toward 1.0. As long as the chance creation holds, +4 is probably below what this side is. If the two numbers hold instead, it is structure, not luck.
  2. The edge-of-the-box concession falls back. If it does not, there is a real problem in that zone.
  3. A comeback arrives. Zero wins from seven is worse than 1.04 xG per second half deserves. If it stays at zero, the surge only works with a lead.
  4. Extra points come from the first half. They will arrive in proportion to how far 0.61 moves toward 1.04.

Appendix — sources, definitions, limits

What the data does not say

  • 26 matches is a small sample. The sub-group numbers — seven matches behind at half-time, six goals after the 90th, a 3.4-goal overshoot at the box edge — are all within the range chance produces. They are described as patterns, not conclusions.
  • No causation was established. The second-half surge survives controlling for game state, but whether it is substitutions, conditioning, a tactical switch or opponents fading cannot be answered here.
  • The size of the reliance on Lee Dong-gyeong was not measured. He has missed one match.
  • The portal defines the same metric in more than one way. Ulsan’s shots read 342 in the event feed, 342 in cross-search and 344 in the per-player records; shots on target read 126, 124, 127 and 181 depending on the system. Every league comparison here uses one system throughout, applied identically to all twelve clubs, and its values are successes rather than attempts.
  • xG here is the sum of the portal’s per-shot values. No club-level official xG table was found in this collection, and xG models differ by provider, so these figures should not be compared with xG from elsewhere.
  • There is no tracking or physical data. “They do not press high” is inferred from the coordinates of defensive actions, not measured from a defensive line.
