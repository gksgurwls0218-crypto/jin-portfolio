Several Kinds of Football in One Match

Part 1 worked out, through mechanics, how a Signature Move works. What it left open is why now, and why for every player. Position-specific fundamentals are built on the assumption that each position receives the ball in one kind of context. That assumption is breaking in four directions at once, and the gap it leaves cannot be filled by adding more techniques.

Starting pointThe question Part 1 left

What position-specific fundamentals assumed

In Part 1 I argued that escaping a press is a time-budget problem, and that as the opponent gets better the decisive question shifts from “how much does it deceive” to “how cheap is this movement for me”. And that this value cannot be set from the outside.

But there was a question it did not answer. So why now? And why for every player? Isn’t it enough for a handful of crack players to have one?

The answer starts with what position-specific fundamentals took for granted. The centre-back receives deep, facing forward. The full-back receives wide, with the touchline at his back. The winger receives outside, facing in. Fundamentals training was designed on the premise one position = one context, drilling the movements used most often in that context.

That premise is now breaking in four directions at once.

Crack 1Where the press happens

The place where a man is on you has moved back

First, a correction. For a long time I have written that “man-oriented high pressing has become the standard”. Having gathered the data, that sentence is an overstatement.

Teams spend most of their time without the ball inside a block. Pressing that follows people is a minority phase. One report even has man-marking at corners falling from 38% to 14%. There is no basis for saying the total amount of man-marking has increased.

But look at location instead of total, and the story changes.

Build-up starts deep and short more often, and the moment it does, people get pressed more often. And what gets marked depends on the height of the block.

Man-marking has not increased. The positions that learned the least on the ball now stand in the box where a man is on you.
Crack 2Separate shapes in and out of possession

One player, two shapes

The second crack is shape. Modern teams defend in one shape and attack in another, and one player does different jobs in the two. This is no longer a special tactic; it is close to the default.

Manchester City defend in a 4-4-2 and build up in a 3-2-5. Stones stepped up from centre-back to a pivot next to Rodri. Full-backs come inside and become midfielders. Here is what that change asks of one player.

When a full-back comes inside, the receiving direction changes (facing forward → back to goal). The receiving height changes (defensive third → middle third). The density around him changes (the side the touchline used to cover opens up). The position’s name stays the same, but his on-ball problem becomes a midfielder’s problem.

Crack 3Mixing blocks

One match, several blocks

The third crack is the opponent. The block a player faces differs not only from match to match but within a single match.

Coaching already teaches it this way. CoachesVoice’s explainer on the high press puts it like this — “Sustaining a committed high press at maximum intensity for 90 minutes is nearly impossible. The most effective pressing teams vary their press with the game state, temporarily dropping into a more conservative shape when tired, when protecting a late lead, or when the opponent’s quality makes pressing too big a risk.” At the Olympics, Morocco, 2-0 up in the second half, committed fewer players forward and added rest defence.

To see whether that is an exception or the norm, I counted across every match of World Cup 2026.

Most teams have two main phases and mix in one or two short ones. From the side with the ball, that means meeting two or three defences of different character within the same 90 minutes.

Crack 4What I measured myself

And that height moves during the match

The World Cup data are 90-minute totals, so they cannot show when things change within a match. So I measured it directly from raw K League events in five matches.

The method is simple. For each team, average the locations of defensive actions (tackles, interceptions, blocks, recoveries, duels, clearances) in each 15-minute window. It is a proxy for the height at which that team was defending.

The lines clearly swing. But with only a few dozen defensive actions per window, chance alone could produce this much wobble. So I reshuffled each team’s defensive actions randomly across windows 2,000 times and compared the real range with the shuffled one.

Defensive height does move within a match. A hypothesis follows naturally — drop off when leading, push up when trailing. Some matches do look like that. When Seoul went 2-0 up, Seoul’s height fell and Incheon’s rose. Suwon also dropped in the last window after coming from behind.

But pool all five matches and split by score state, and this is what you get.

What this tells us — height moves. But the reason is not the score alone. Fatigue, time, substitutions, changes by the opponent and the flow of the match are all mixed in. From the player’s point of view, what matters is the result, not the reason — within the same match, the height at which he receives and the distance of the opponent’s press change by tens of metres.
MultiplyPutting the four cracks together

How many contexts one player faces

The four cracks do not add — they multiply. Two shapes mean two receiving spots; on top of that come three opponent blocks; and their height moves during the match.

ObjectionThen why not just add techniques?

Can you build one technique per context?

The natural answer: if there are six boxes, teach one technique for each. One turn with your back to goal, one change of direction in front of the block, one way to progress through the half-space.

There are two problems.

First, when the task changes, the movement changes. Horst and colleagues (2020) tried to identify people from the throwing movements of seven decathletes. Within the same event the body’s fingerprint was clear, but identifying an athlete from a different event’s movement reached at most 52.6%. Identifying which event a movement came from, on the other hand, was 100%. The task reshapes the movement a great deal. Build a separate technique for each box, and you have to take each of the six to the automatic stage.

Second, under pressure only one comes out. That was Part 1’s conclusion — under pressure, explicitly learned movements fall back into conscious control and break down, and the movement the body judges cheapest comes up. Of six techniques, the one you can actually pull out under pressure is the one most deeply ingrained in your body.

Overlay the two results and the answer appears. Change the task and close to half of the movement changes. The other half the person carries with him — a way of moving that is his alone and survives both fatigue and a change of task.

That carried half is the Signature Move.

ConclusionWhy it is inevitable

What crosses contexts is not a technique but a person

To sum up.

When positions set the context, position-specific fundamentals were enough. A centre-back only had to solve a centre-back’s problems. Now the centre-back is picked up by a man at the goal kick, the full-back receives with his back to goal in a midfielder’s spot, and the opponent’s block changes two or three times in the same 90 minutes.

The road of filling the extra contexts with more techniques is blocked twice. Change the task and the movement changes, so all six must be ingrained separately; and under pressure only one of them comes out.

One road is left. One movement, most deeply ingrained in that player’s body, that he can carry across contexts. That is why I see the Signature Move not as a crack player’s ornament but as basic equipment every player needs in modern football.

One thing youth development should change

Don’t give players positions to experience. Give them contexts.

Put the same player through the back-to-goal box, the in-front-of-the-block box and the man-on-you box — and, following Part 1’s boundary, change “where you need to go” (L3) for each box while leaving “how you get there” (L4) unspecified. Then the movement that keeps coming out of that player across boxes starts to show. That is what to develop.

⚠ This prescription comes from argument, not from a test. Nobody has yet measured whether a group trained across mixed contexts is stronger under pressure.
CaveatsWhat this piece cannot claim

Written down so as not to overstate

This piece argues for inevitability, but some links in that chain are inference, not measurement. I list them separately.

  • There is no evidence that the total amount of man-marking has increased. Pressing phases are about 19% of out-of-possession time, and many accounts still describe zonal marking as the default. What this piece claims is not a change in total but a shift in where people get marked.
  • Nor is there evidence that mixing blocks pays off. At World Cup 2026 the correlation between low-block shape and winning was −0.13 to +0.17, essentially none. This piece does not say mixing is good — it says mixing is happening, and players have to cope with it.
  • The in-match change in height is an observation from five K League matches. The measure also carries the height at which the match was played, not just the choice of block, and it was not explained by score state.
  • “Contexts × N” and the full-back grid are conceptual diagrams. How many contexts one player actually visits in a match cannot be measured without tracking data.
  • Cross-task transfer (52.6%) comes from closed skills in seven decathletes. There is no evidence that the same ratio holds for on-ball football.

ClosingOne sentence linking Parts 1 and 2

Positions no longer set the context

Part 1 said that under pressure, the movement the body judges cheapest comes out. Part 2 says that modern football creates those moments of pressure in more contexts, more often.

Put together: a player prepared with position-specific fundamentals has nothing to pull out when he is under pressure outside his position’s box — and those moments keep increasing. A player who has one movement he can carry across contexts pulls out the same thing whichever box he is rushed in.

He does not have to be a crack player. One is enough. But that one has to come from that player’s own body, and has to survive across contexts.

Positions no longer set the context. So what crosses contexts has to be the person.
