BASELINE29 matches · How results were decided

Half their leads were wiped out, and none was won back

Bucheon took the lead in 14 of their 29 matches. In half of them — seven — that lead was wiped out, and not once did they go back in front afterwards: 0 wins, 5 draws, 2 defeats. The seven leads that survived are the season’s seven wins.

Six of the seven wins came the same way: score first, never concede an equaliser. The one exception is the opening day (R1, 3–2 away at Jeonbuk). Bucheon were behind twice, at 0–1 and 1–2, and won it with a penalty seven minutes into stoppage time. It is their only comeback win of the season, and the only time they were ahead in that match was right at the end.

So the whole season hangs on the first goal.

Once they are pegged back, that is the end of it. This piece asks why.
TWISTExpected vs actual

First, this side is weaker than the table says

Before answering, the baseline needs setting. 32 points and tenth place are not a straight reading of this team’s level.

Across 29 matches, expected goals for are 27.5 and expected goals against 45.8. Expected goal difference −18.3; actual −7. That is 11.3 goals gained on expectation, and 7.8 of them at the defensive end.

A defensive overperformance of 7.8 goals (11.5 on the 28 observed matches) is large. If it is skill, Bucheon are doing something xG cannot capture. If it is variance, it will be gone next season. The two readings point in opposite directions, so I tested it three ways.

TESTDefensive overperformance of 11.5 goals

Luck or skill — three checks

One — it is not the goalkeeper

Two goalkeepers shared the season. If one man were performing above himself, the two should look different.

Their save rates are 2.1 points apart. The excess did not split by save rate but in proportion to how much xGA each faced (31.63 → +8.63; 12.88 → +2.88). Against the opposing goalkeepers it is 70.8% vs 68.8% — a 2.0-point edge is nowhere near big enough to produce 11.5 goals.

Two — it is not the defensive structure either

The control group is FC Anyang, parsed through the same pipeline: same league, same season, 29 matches.

Anyang block 2.0 points more, and the on-target gap is only 4.2 points. Yet the defensive overperformance is +11.5 for Bucheon and −3.3 for Anyang. If almost the same suppression produces opposite results, suppression cannot be the cause.

A correction to my own calculation. During the analysis I wrote that “if opponents hit the target as often as Bucheon do, 79% of the excess is explained.” I used the wrong baseline. The comparison should not be Bucheon’s own attacking on-target rate (35.9%) but what other teams actually allow. Using Anyang (32.0%) as the baseline, the gap is 4.2 points and the expected extra goals conceded fall to 5.0. That is 43%, not 79%.

Three — a variance test, and the missing match

I treated each of the 408 opposition shots as a coin flip weighted by its xG and simulated the season 200,000 times.

On 28 matches it is narrowly significant, p = 0.026. But those 28 matches are the sample with the 0–5 defeat taken out. Put R27 back under any reasonable assumption and p rises to 0.07–0.11. The defence looks good partly because its worst match is missing, and this test was exactly the one most exposed to that bias.

In short, the repeatable part is at most around four goals; the other seven or so cannot be separated from variance with this data. The safer reading is that Bucheon are weaker than 32 points. Which leaves one question — why is the attack only this good?

DIAGNOSISFinal-third entries

They cannot get the ball forward

Once in the final third, Bucheon shoot readily — 13.21 shots per 100 final-third passes, the highest of the four groups compared (opponents 11.12, Anyang 11.27). The problem is how often they arrive: 22.2 vs 33.2 successful entries a match, 67% of the opposition’s figure.

A 51.1% success rate is lower not only than Bucheon’s opponents (60.8%) but also than Anyang (56.6%) and Anyang’s opponents (62.9%). Split the 9.8-point gap by method and more than half of it comes from one cell.

Going long is not the weakness. Long-pass success is level with the opposition, 40.5% vs 41.7%. What Bucheon do not use is the middle distance — and long passes (43.5%) and free kicks (12.6%, 2.1 times the opposition’s share) fill the space it leaves.

How rarely they play the mid-range pass

Failure concentrates in the passes meant to go forward. Mid-range forward passes into the final third: 298, completed 56.0% (opponents 500, 70.4%). Those that end inside the middle third: 647, 80.8% (opponents 1,279, 90.5%). The safe pass mostly works; the one meant to progress is where it breaks down.
CORE238 central entries

The centre was never closed

By lane, the centre stands out. Bucheon complete 32.8% of their central entries; their opponents 48.0%, Anyang 46.0%, Anyang’s opponents 44.7%. Bucheon alone are in the thirties; the other three sit between 44 and 48%.

It looks blocked. Open up how the ball goes in, and a different picture appears.

A short pass into the centre succeeds 78.0% of the time. For the opposition it is 79.7%. Almost identical.

That single line is the whole section. When Bucheon play a short pass into the centre, it gets through as often as anyone else’s. The centre is not closed. They simply use that route for only 17.2% of central entries (opponents 22.2%). The rest is long passes (42.0%) and free kicks (18.9%), which come off one time in five.

Mid-range passes into the centre also split, 36.5% vs 61.6% — a 25.1-point gap. The cell from the previous section turns up again here.

LANESLeft, centre and right in the final third

Leaning left is not the mistake

Bucheon’s entries lean left: 44.8% (opponents 38.7%). So do 47.3% of their final-third passes and 52.1% of their crosses. It is tempting to read the bias itself as the problem. The output says otherwise.

Bucheon turn wide entries into shots more often than their opponents do — 16.7% vs 12.3% on the left, 16.0% vs 13.8% on the right. Going left is not the wrong choice.

The loss is in the centre. Successful central entries number 78 against 160 — half — and the xG they produce is 1.31 vs 3.46. A 2.15 difference from a single lane.

There is one cost here. Bucheon take 16.5% of their shots from the left flank (opponents 10.0%). Those 43 shots add up to 1.5 xG — 0.035 each. This is the second reason their xG per shot is low: 0.0995 against the opposition’s 0.1091.

And the few that do end from further out

Split shots by what happened just before them, and one cell stands out.

The recovery spot is almost the same — the median X of the defensive action before those shots is 75.2 vs 76.9. They win the ball in the same place, but only Bucheon shoot from further out (median distance 20.1 m vs 18.7 m; 61.0% vs 53.3% from beyond 18 m).

Counterfactual. If only the post-recovery shots matched the opposition’s quality (0.1182), season xG would rise from 25.8 to 28.5 and xG per shot from 0.0995 to 0.1101 — above the opposition’s 0.1091. The xG-per-shot gap is, in effect, this one cell.

The same finger points at the same place here too — within three seconds of winning the ball, Bucheon choose a mid-range forward pass 2.4% vs 3.9% of the time.

RETURNGame state

Which is why level is their worst state

Everything above comes together in xG by game state.

As ratios: leading 0.54 · level 0.53 · trailing 0.78. They drop off when ahead or level, and only come out when behind. And the default state of a match is level — which makes 0.53 this team’s baseline.

Less a side that protects leads than one that is pushed back just as hard ahead as level (xG ratio 0.54 vs 0.53).

DISCIPLINEHypothesis log

What did not survive testing

Five hypotheses raised during this analysis did not survive the data. All five were mine. They stay in — a rejected hypothesis marks the edges of the conclusions that did survive.

✕

The 11.5-goal defensive overperformance belongs to the goalkeeper

Rejected. The two goalkeepers’ save rates are 70.1% and 72.2%, 2.1 points apart. The excess split by how much xGA each faced, not by save rate. Against the opposing goalkeepers the edge is only 2.0 points (70.8% vs 68.8%).

✕

The overperformance comes from a structure that suppresses shots

Rejected. Anyang, parsed through the same pipeline, actually block more (21.5%) and the on-target gap is only 4.2 points — yet the excess is +11.5 for Bucheon and −3.3 for Anyang. If almost the same suppression produces opposite results, suppression cannot be the cause.

✕

An 8-point gap in opposition on-target rate explains 79% of the excess

Corrected. My own calculation, and I used the wrong baseline — Bucheon’s own attacking on-target rate (35.9%). Using what other teams actually allow (Anyang, 32.0%), the gap is 4.2 points, the expected extra goals 5.0 — that is, 43%.

✕

Bucheon’s shots come from scrappy situations, so they are poor chances

Rejected. Bucheon’s xG per shot is 0.1073 in settled situations (after a completed pass) and 0.1064 in scrappy ones (after a duel or a loose ball) — no difference. They do have more scrappy shots (29.7% vs 19.9%), but that does not lower the quality in itself. The one thing that does is shooting from distance after a recovery.

✕

The left-side bias (44.8%) makes the attack one-dimensional

Rejected. Bucheon actually convert wide entries into shots more often (left 16.7% vs 12.3%, right 16.0% vs 13.8%). The loss is in the centre, not on the flank — 78 vs 160 successful entries, xG 1.31 vs 3.46.

Three traps in the data. ① The portal has no events for R27 (0–5 vs Daejeon). Its official 45-metric table leaves the same match out while still reporting 29 matches played. Drop the heaviest defeat and the defensive numbers look better than they are — one of the tests in this piece flipped on exactly that bias. ② Own-goal attribution is reversed between sources. The scoring-situation table credits the team that benefits; the event feed tags the team of the player who kicked it. Without flipping it, two goals go missing and you end up hunting a mis-tag that does not exist. ③ The portal’s “passes” counts completed passes only (7,062, not 9,544 attempts).
PRESCRIPTIONPriorities

Where to start, and what is better left alone

The diagnosis has narrowed to “they do not use the route into the centre”, so that is where intervention starts. The order below is lowest cost first — not signings or a new formation, but changing the choices of players already in the squad.

1

Play short into the centre more often

A short pass into the centre succeeds 78.0% of the time, effectively level with the opposition (79.7%). This is a question of frequency, not ability — only 17.2% of central entries use it, with long passes (42.0%) and free kicks (18.9%) making up the rest. Those two succeed 19.0% and 17.8% of the time.

Central xG (1.31) moves with every central entry added to the current 78. The opposition’s equivalent figure is 3.46.

Evidence · short central entries 78.0% vs 79.7% · central entry success of 32.8%, lowest of the four groups
2

Play more mid-range forward passes

33.8 a match, 53% of the opposition’s 63.5. There is more room in raising the volume than in raising the 73.0% completion rate. Long-pass success is already level with the opposition.

The players whose passes arrive are already in the squad — Ahn Tae-hyun 41.4%, Kim Jong-woo 41.2%, Basani 38.7% — yet they attempt only 29, 34 and 31.

Evidence · 33.8 vs 63.5 per match · more than half of the 9.8-point entry gap sits in this cell
3

After winning the ball, carry it one step further before shooting

The recovery spot is almost the same as the opposition’s (X 75.2 vs 76.9). But the median shot distance is 20.1 m vs 18.7 m, and 61.0% vs 53.3% of these shots come from beyond 18 m.

On the counterfactual, bringing this one cell up to the opposition’s quality takes xG per shot from 0.0995 to 0.1101 — above the opposition. The xG-per-shot gap is, in effect, this cell.

Evidence · xG per shot after a recovery 0.0848 vs 0.1182 · 31.7% of all shots
4

Review the roles of the two most-used attackers

Gallego (FW, 2,544 min) and Ahn Tae-hyun (MF, 2,244 min) attempt 1.13 and 1.16 mid-range forward passes per 90, near the bottom of the squad. Gallego’s main position is forward, so few passes starting in the middle third is natural in itself. The issue is less the passing frequency than where his possession leads — 14.33 touches per 90 on the left of the final third, 1.9 times the next player, without that possession turning into central entries.

But this data cannot tell whether it is a question of positioning or of instructions. It needs checking on video.

Evidence · MF/FW top two by minutes: 1.13 and 1.16 attempts per 90 · top three for arrival rate made 29–34 attempts

Better left alone

✕

Finishing in the final third

13.21 shots per 100 final-third passes, the highest of the four groups compared (opponents 11.12 · Anyang 11.27 · Anyang’s opponents 10.45). Shooting once they arrive already works.

✕

The left-side bias

Wide entries actually convert to shots more often than the opposition’s (left 16.7% vs 12.3%). The task is to add the centre, not to take away the left.

✕

Winning the ball more often

72.8 recoveries a match, already 23% more than the opposition (59.0). What needs changing is the location, not the volume (median X 18.5 vs 26.0).

✕

Counting the defensive structure as an achievement

Of the +11.5-goal defensive overperformance, at most around four goals look repeatable. Plan next season as if all of it were skill and the baseline is inflated by 7–8 goals.
