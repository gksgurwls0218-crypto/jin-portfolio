WHAT THE DATA SAYS · 05

The risk they never bought,
the chances they never got

Jeju SK absorb organised attacks better than anyone in K League 1. They also create almost nothing themselves. Twenty-one of their 27 matches were decided by one goal or none — and that was not an accident, it was a choice.

K League 1 2026 · rounds 1–27Han Hyuck-jin9 Sep 2026 Source · K League Data Portal

WHAT THIS PIECE SAYS

  1. Jeju concede from opponents’ slow build at 0.44× the expected rate — best in the league. Only fast breaks get through (42% of goals conceded).
  2. Those breaks do not start with a long ball. Of the 11 goals conceded on the counter, the goalkeeper originated zero.
  3. They start with a short forward pass in the defensive third. That single cell is 12 points worse than opponents; nowhere else is.
  4. Adjust for location and player differences remain. One man is cleared of a charge; four are not.
  5. Going long is free. It also yields 0.061 shots per phase. That is what nine draws cost.

00How narrow the season was

What this team’s matches actually look like

Twenty-one of Jeju SK’s 27 matches were decided by one goal or none. Nine draws, twelve one-goal games. Only six were settled by two goals or more.

The nine draws read 0-0 four times, 1-1 four times, 3-3 once. Eight of them finished with two goals or fewer on the board.

None of that is chance. The narrowness is chosen. This piece works out what the choice costs.

01Jeju are the exact inverse of Jeonbuk

Same rounds, same portal, same coordinate frame

Episode 04 covered Jeonbuk Hyundai. The datasets line up exactly, so the two can be laid on top of each other.

02And yet they concede an average number

Why is a low-possession, low-press side 9th for goals against?

Eleventh for passes. A PPDA of 6.5. Eleventh for exposure-normalised defensive volume. How many goals should a side with all three concede? Twenty-seven in 27 matches — ninth in the league. Average. With nine clean sheets.

The raw defensive counts do not explain it. Interceptions 5th, blocks 6th, tackles 7th — nothing that dominates the league. This is not a side that defends by covering ground.

So what holds their goals-against at the league average? The answer is not how much they defend but what they defend.

03They absorb slow build better than anyone

And only the counter gets through

Opponent attacks were split by phase type: fast break (three passes or fewer and 15 seconds or less), slow build (eight passes or more), everything else middle.

Deny them time and they break. So how does an opponent come to have no time?
There is only one way — Jeju have to give the ball away.

04Which is where I got it wrong

One definition, and the whole conclusion moves

Here is what the first draft of this piece concluded, verbatim.

Of the 653 times Jeju lost the ball in their own last third, 33 became an opponent shot within 20 seconds. A 5.1% conversion — five times the rate of any other zone. Both sides make the same mistake; only Jeju are punished for it.

A tidy story. It was also wrong.

The problem was the definition of “losing the ball”. I had counted failed passes plus lost ground duels plus lost aerial duels. Losing a header in your own box as a cross comes in is not losing the ball — the opponent already had it. That is a defensive failure, not a turnover, and counting it as one turns the finding into a tautology: “they lost a duel just before conceding, therefore they conceded.”

Splitting Jeju’s 129 duel losses in their own last third by who held the ball in the previous three events: 118 of them (91%) were never turnovers at all — and five of the seven goals hung on those 118.

This chapter stays in because a single definition moving an entire conclusion is the lesson this series keeps relearning. The coordinate frame was re-checked separately — Jeju’s goals average X 90.7, opponents’ 87.8, both sides’ goalkeeper passes average X 11.0 and 11.9. Restore the original definition and the first draft’s 653·33·7 and 566·25·1 reproduce exactly. The arithmetic was right; the definition was not.

05The obvious suspect

And the most clearly innocent

Delete the wrong answer and the question reopens. Where do those 11 counter goals come from?

The first suspect was the goalkeeper. 432 of Kim Dong-jun’s 811 passes (53.3%) travel more than 30 metres. 45.1% of them arrive. And of Jeju’s 653 losses in their own last third, 221 — 34% — came off his boot.

On the numbers alone he looks guilty. But those numbers mean nothing without something to compare them to. Is 45% high or low?

So I built a league benchmark. The Jeju, Jeonbuk and Ulsan datasets each contain both teams’ events; deduplicated by match id they merge into 73 matches, 105,271 events, 4,343 goalkeeper passes.

34% and 45.1% are different numbers. 45.1% is his completion rate on passes over 30 metres (fourth in the league). 34% is his share of Jeju’s 653 losses in their own last third (221 of them). And that 34% counts occurrences, not damage.

6-3 · Which closes the goalkeeper’s story

Chapter 05 noted that Kim Dong-jun plays 379 passes under 30 metres and completes 98.9% of them. Each of those 379 was followed for five events to see what became of it.

This is why he kicks half of them long. The short ball does not come back. Chapter 05 acquitted him because his long passes do not lead to goals. Here is the second half of it — when he does play short, his team cannot hold it.

6-4 · Player or place — a verdict

All of the above is about location. Does the man standing there matter at all?

The split was done by indirect standardisation. The pitch is divided into 18 cells (three thirds × three channels × two directions), and for each player the question is “how many passes should he have lost, if his mix of positions and directions had been played at the team’s average rate?” The reference rate excludes the player himself, so nobody sets his own benchmark. Actual failures divided by that expectation is O/E.

The answer: location first, but the player survives it. Three things are settled.

One — Kim Ryun-seong is cleared. His raw failure rate in his own half was 15.4%, among the highest in the squad. But he takes most of his forward passes from the deep left corner, the hardest square on the pitch for this team. Adjust for it and he sits at O/E 0.89 (0.65–1.21) — exactly where he should be.

Two — Celestin and Tobias are genuinely good at it. 0.38 and 0.52. From the same squares they lose it at less than half the team rate. Celestin’s upper bound is 0.59 across 342 attempts.

Three — Negueba’s 2.94 needs an explanation. 219 attempts in the build-up zone, 16 expected failures, 47 actual. Neither location nor position accounts for it.

One variable is not controlled — the difficulty of the pass attempted. Within the same cell, a safe ball sideways and a pass that breaks a line are different acts, and coordinates plus direction cannot tell them apart. Negueba takes 26% of this team’s shots on his own. He is also the player most likely to attempt the risky pass.

So the accurate way to put it is this — Negueba’s losses in the build-up zone are not explained by where he stands or what position he plays. Whether that is technique or the risk he accepts cannot be settled without measuring pass difficulty.

In episode 04 I found, late, that a finishing failure I had pinned on players was in fact attached to a phase. This time it runs the other way. Adjust for location and one man walks free, four do not.

07The risk they never bought

Does the avoidance come free?

The same phase split, applied to Jeju’s own attacks.

Going long is free. It originates none of the goals conceded on the counter.
It also produces nothing — 0.061 shots per phase.

Playing short is where the goals conceded begin. Four of eleven.
It is also where the shots come from — 0.240 per phase.

Jeju are a team that declined this trade. They never bought the risk, so they never got the chances.

It meshes exactly with episode 04. Jeonbuk bought the chances by playing through, and watched them harden against a low block. Jeju never went to the counter at all.

The result is nine draws. Four 0-0s, four 1-1s. The most common scoreline was 1-2 — five times. That is the scoreline of a team that loses narrowly. The record is the strategy.

08Attack — Jeonbuk’s mirror image

And what the sample will not allow

Episode 04 concluded that Jeonbuk could not finish the chances they made — 36 shots and no goals from slow build. Jeju are the exact opposite.

Fifteen. The 95% confidence interval on 66.7% runs 41.7% to 84.8%. The opponents’ 33.3% runs 15.2% to 58.3% — the two intervals overlap. “Jeju finish better than their opponents” is a sentence this data cannot carry.

That is 0.56 big chances per match. Even at 38 rounds it is about 21. I will recompute this chapter when the season ends — and if it is still 21, I will say that it remains provisional. Finishing is not a skill a single season settles.

One thing the sample does support: Jeju’s attacking problem is not finishing but the sheer number of decisive chances. Of 312 shots, 43.3% are worth less than 0.05 xG (Jeonbuk 31%). They are not short of shots; they are short of places to shoot from.

Negueba alone took 82 of them — 26% of the team’s total — at 0.072 xG per shot against a team average of 0.095. Six goals.

Set pieces, by contrast, are one of the few places this team manufactures anything. Of 107 corners, 19 produced a shot within 15 seconds and 3 produced a goal. Across the merged 73-match sample the league converts 435 corners into 5 goals — 1.1% against Jeju’s 2.8%. Though it is 3 goals; that is not a number to lean on either.

10So what kind of team is this?

  • Less of the ball (11th for passes), a softer press (PPDA 6.5), fewer defensive actions per exposure (11th).
  • And yet they absorb organised attacks better than anyone in the league (0.44×).
  • What beats them is the counter — 42% of goals conceded, at 1.88× the expected rate.
  • Those counters begin not with a long ball but with a failed short pass in their own half. The goalkeeper originates none.
  • One cell carries the flaw — short forward passes in the defensive third (+12.1 points), and inside it, both box corners (56.8% and 51.9%).
  • Adjust for location and players remain: Kim Ryun-seong is cleared; Celestin 0.38, Tobias 0.52 and Negueba 2.94 are not.
  • They finish the chances they make (66.7% of big chances). But there are 15 of them. No verdict.
  • What is certain is the volume — 43% of their shots are worth under 0.05 xG.

Jeju are built not to lose, and the design works.
39 points, fifth, nine clean sheets, unbeaten when they score first.

The same design produced nine draws and seven blanks.
Decline the risk and you decline the chances with it. That is the bill for this season.

Open questions

  • Is Negueba’s 2.94 technique or accepted risk? The remaining variable is the difficulty of the pass attempted, and coordinates plus direction cannot separate a safe ball from one that breaks a line. Answering it needs data on the receiver and the opposing defensive shape.
  • Do opponents know about this? Whether pressing lines rise specifically against Jeju, or whether Jeju are cut off in the same square by everyone — that needs a match-by-match split.
  • Does the rate of losses in the last third change with the score state (ahead, level, behind)?
  • How the 15 big chances were generated — alongside the end-of-season recount.
  • What second place for offsides means (48, mean X 69.3).
  • Is conceding 0.87× expected goalkeeping? → This cannot be answered. The data has no post-shot xG.
  • The home/away split (17 against 10) — too lopsided a sample to answer.

Method

  • Data — K League Data Portal. Every event from Jeju’s rounds 1–27, both teams included. The goalkeeper and corner benchmarks merge the Jeju, Jeonbuk and Ulsan datasets, deduplicated by match id (73 matches, 105,271 events). Sample sizes differ by club — 27 matches each for Jeju, Ulsan and Jeonbuk, six to nine for the rest.
  • Pass length units — verified as metres against coordinate distance (median ratio 1.000, median absolute error 0.03m, n=24,101).
  • Phase reconstruction — possession changes hands on a sustained turnover; a single opponent touch does not end a phase. Fast break = three passes or fewer and 15 seconds or less; slow build = eight passes or more; the rest is middle.
  • Definition of losing the ball — failed passes only (PSU/MISS). Lost duels (GDL/ADL) are excluded, for the reason set out in chapter 04. Sensitivity was computed under all three definitions.
  • Coordinate frame — X runs in each team’s attacking direction (own goal at X=0), verified against mean goal X and mean goalkeeper-pass X. Thirds are defensive 0–33.3, middle 33.3–66.7, final 66.7–100.
  • Channels — low Y is the left. Fixed in episode 04 by cross-checking match-report zone codes against goal-event Y (97.1% agreement on paired comparisons).
  • Outfield classification — anyone ever named as a starting goalkeeper is excluded; everyone else takes their modal non-substitute position.
  • Player O/E — indirect standardisation across 18 cells (third × channel × direction). The expectation excludes the player himself (leave-one-out); intervals are Byar’s approximation. Heterogeneity χ² is decomposed into between- and within-position components.
  • Goal counts — the official record is 31 scored and 27 conceded; the event data’s shot-goals are 30 and 26. The difference is two goals mis-tagged ST/RCV by the portal (R20 Gwangju Jeong Ji-hun, 0.710 xG; R23 Jeju Yoo In-soo, 0.225 xG), confirmed by a round-by-round reconciliation. Phase and xG analysis uses the event-level 30 and 26.

Corrections

① The turnover axis — the first draft was built on “losses in the last third are five times more dangerous” and “the same mistake, a different price (653 losses and 7 goals against 566 and 1)”. Both came from including lost duels in the definition of losing the ball, and both were discarded. Chapter 04 keeps the record.

② Corners — the first draft said “107 corners, no goals”. That was wrong. The portal’s goal descriptions tag penalties and free kicks but not corners, and the absence was read as a zero. Counted directly from events it is three goals within 15 seconds — 2.5× the league rate. The conclusion inverted.

③ Position categories — the team sheet records a real position only for starters; every substitute is filed as “bench” (484 of 1,078 rows). Taken at face value, “bench” becomes a position. Players were reassigned to their modal non-substitute position.

④ Defensive action rankings — the first draft’s “3rd for clearances, 3rd for blocks, 1st for aerials” could not be reproduced; those fields are not in the portal’s aggregate search. What is verifiable is 5th for interceptions, 6th for blocks, 7th for tackles — none of which is a top-of-the-league figure, so the claim was removed.

⑤ The Big-5 comparison — not done. FBref has withdrawn its advanced goalkeeper data; Launch% and AvgLen are empty for 2023-24, 2024-25 and 2025-26 alike. Even with the numbers the definitions do not line up — FBref’s Launch% is over 40 yards and excludes goal kicks, where this piece uses 30m and 40m thresholds and includes them. A league-internal benchmark on one source is the more honest comparison.
