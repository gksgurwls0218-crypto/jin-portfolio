FC SEOUL 2026 · ROUNDS 1–25 · ATTACKING PHASE

Everyone gets the ball
to the same place

FC Seoul are a team of five-goal days and no-goal days. But getting the ball into the central area in front of goal looked the same on both. The difference came after that, and the public data could not explain it. This is a record of how far the numbers go, where they stop, and what should be measured at the point where they stop.

Hyukjin HanAugust 202634,518 events across 25 matchesSource: K League Data Portal

THE SHORT VERSION

  1. Of the 43 goals scored by Seoul players themselves, 40 came from one cell — the centre of the box (zone 17). But the share of box entries reaching that cell is 72% for Seoul and 69% for the eleven opponents. Nothing distinguishes Seoul up to that point.
  2. The difference is what happens after arrival. Ranking all 25 matches by the xG each delivery produced, the top ten went 10W 0D 0L and the bottom ten 3W 5D 2L.
  3. And the side that delivered more scored one fifth as many goals — 20.7 deliveries and 33 goals against 30.0 deliveries and 6 goals.
  4. I tested every attribute the event data holds: where the cross came from, where it landed, its type, the structure preceding it. Top and bottom matches look nearly identical. Only the outcome differs, fourfold.
  5. The remaining variable exists only on video — whether the player was free at the moment of delivery. The piece closes with a way to measure it.

01The question

Why does the same team score five one week and none the next?

FC Seoul scored 47 goals through round 25: 43 from open play, 2 from free-kicks, 1 penalty and 1 opposition own goal. The spread between matches is wide. A side that won 7-0 at Anyang (R24) and 5-0 at home to Gwangju (R05) also failed to score in four matches against Jeonbuk, Gangwon, Gimcheon and Anyang.

In the 16 matches where they scored first, Seoul went 15W 1D 0L. In the five where they conceded first, 1W 4L. All four goalless matches were draws. This team wins whenever it scores. So the question narrows to one thing: what is different about the days they score?

This piece covers the attacking phase only. I also tested the defensive transition, but once match status is controlled for, matches with more crosses actually conceded fewer goals (r = −0.298). The problem was not conceding; it was scoring. That is why the scope was narrowed. The verification is documented separately.

02A first hypothesis, and its rejection

"Seoul use the middle of the box well" — the data said no

Watching the matches, my first read was this: Seoul get bodies into the middle of the box well. R09, the 3-0 win over Bucheon, was the clearest case — four players arriving in front of goal at once in the 84th minute.

To test that, "the middle of the box" has to become a coordinate. On the standard 18-zone grid — six columns by three rows — the central cell in front of goal is called zone 17: X ≥ 83.3, Y between 33.3 and 66.7.

Seoul's goals do cluster in that one cell: 40 of 43 (93%). So do Seoul use it better than others?

No. Of 834 balls Seoul played into the box, 602 (72%) landed in zone 17. The eleven opponents managed 438 of 636 (69%). Computed team by team rather than pooled, the opposition average is 68.6% — the same. The xG generated per delivery matches to three decimal places: 0.050 against 0.050.

Getting the ball into the centre of the box is not a distinguishing act in this league. The first hypothesis fails here. And yet the outcomes are not the same — from that cell Seoul took 163 shots for 40 goals, their opponents 122 shots for 16.

If everyone gets the ball there, the difference is in what happens next.

03Ranking the 25 matches

xG per delivery — the top ten won every match, the bottom ten scored six goals in ten

One metric. xG per delivery = the xG created from zone 17 in that match, divided by the number of balls delivered into it. It asks how dangerous each individual delivery into the centre of the box was, rather than how many there were.

R05, the 5-0 against Gwangju, put the ball into the centre of the box 17 times. R20, the 1-3 against Ulsan, did it 50 times — nearly three times as often, for one fifth of the goals.

The third row is the reason. How many times the ball is delivered within a single attack is what differs.

On the good days one delivery ends the attack — a shot, or a turnover. On the bad days it goes in, gets cleared, is picked up and goes in again, and is cleared again. The cycle repeats inside the same possession. The delivery count accumulates while shots do not, and the shots that do come are taken from poor angles. The share of shots with at least a 20% chance of going in is 47% against 12% — four times apart.

Just over a minute around the 87th in the Pohang match shows the shape of it. The ball went into the same place three times — a corner from the left, a corner from the right, and an open-play cross. The first two were cleared; Kim Jin-su won the second ball from the second corner, four players moved it round, and Lee Seung-mo delivered a third time for the goal.

But the official highlight keeps only the last fifteen seconds. The two corners and the two clearances are gone, so on video the sequence reads as "defend, break out, cross, score". Knowing the preceding 49 seconds makes it "scored at the third attempt".

Highlights keep the last action and discard what came before. From public video alone there is no way to show a failed repetition.

04The best of them — R05, Gwangju at home, 5-0

17 deliveries, 6 shots, 5 goals. And all five arrived by a different route

R05, the Gwangju match on 22 March, ranks third by xG per delivery and second in the season for xG per shot, at 0.304. Yet its 17 deliveries put it level with the lower-ranked matches. This was not a win built on volume.

All five goals finished in the same cell. The way there was different each time. Classifying the final delivery gives four types — short pass, open cross, set-piece cross, long throw-in. This is the only match in the 25 to use all four. R24, the 7-0 at Anyang, used three, and five of its six goals came from open crosses.

Not a match won by delivering into the centre often, but one that arrived there five different ways.

05But why it happened is not explained

I put in every attribute the data holds, and they all came back the same

So far, what differed. The next question is why. I tested each attribute the event data carries for anything separating the good crosses from the rest.

First, the three structures that are supposed to create freedom, each tagged individually: a switch (a cross played straight after a large change of side), a three-player combination (the previous three passes involving three different players), and an individual beat (a cross straight after the crosser's own successful dribble). All 436 open-play crosses were classified.

How set pieces were removed from the sample. From round 19 the portal records set-piece crosses under the open-play code (through round 18 they carried their own). I confirmed it by checking the coordinates of corner-position deliveries, and since coordinates alone cannot isolate free-kicks, any cross more than 15 seconds after the previous event was treated as a dead ball and excluded. On rounds 1–18, where the set-piece code still exists, that rule produces zero false positives. That removes 47 of 483, leaving the 436 used in this section.

Only the three-player combination points weakly in the expected direction. The real problem came next.

The crosses from the top and bottom matches cannot be told apart. The share preceded by structure, the share of cut-backs, the accuracy of arrival, the position they were played from — all effectively equal. And yet the xG each cross generates is 0.0700 against 0.0167, a factor of four.

There is one case that makes this unmistakable.

Four crosses Choi Jun played to Klimala from the same place on the right. Same deliverer, same receiver, same origin coordinate, same side, same space. Two came in matches Seoul won (R03 Jeju away 2-1, R09 Bucheon home 3-0) and two in matches they did not (R17 Gangwon home 0-0, R12 Anyang home 0-0). The event data offers no way to separate them.

06So what should be measured

Recording the quality of a cross as the freedom the player had when he played it

There are three metrics for crossing: attempted, completed, completion rate. But completion rate asks only "did it reach a teammate", never "did reaching him create danger". Adding coordinates changed nothing. A fourth axis is needed.

The proposal is to tag the room the player had at the moment of delivery on three axes.

Axis2 points1 point0 points
F1 PressureNo opponent within 3mOpponent 3–7m and closingOpponent within 3m, or contact
F2 TimeTwo touches or from a standstillOne touch firstFirst time / off a loose ball
F3 Range of optionsCut-back, cut inside and recycle all availableOne alternativeNo option other than the cross
A note on F3. This axis measures the range of options, not the quality of the choice. A zero does not mean the cross was a bad decision. It means there was no alternative, and a cross may well be the best thing to do in that situation. The score therefore describes the situation rather than judging the player. A well-struck forced cross stays on record as F3 zero with a good outcome, and that is itself information.

Five or six points is a free cross, zero to two a pressured cross. Corners and free-kicks are structurally unpressured and must be counted separately. The 3m and 7m thresholds on F1 fill a box that FIFA's Football Language leaves at "appropriate distance" without a number; the analyst sets it, and it is labelled as such.

And the tagging leads to a prescription

For Seoul's bottom ten matches the vertical axis already exists — 0.0162 xG per cross. The horizontal axis is empty, so the cell cannot be identified. Top-left (freedom wasted) prescribes crossing technique and decision work; bottom-left (both failing) prescribes rebuilding the build-up. They are opposites.

The framework also defines the condition that would kill it. If the shot-conversion rate of free crosses and pressured crosses turns out to be the same, the concept is wrong and another axis has to be found.

Cost. A match averages 19.3 open-play crosses. At roughly 40 seconds per cross across three axes, that is about 15 minutes per match — a single day to re-tag all 25. It needs video and a recording sheet, nothing else.

Every number here comes from public data, and I watched and recorded the matches myself. The one constraint was that the video work could only be done from YouTube highlights, since full-match footage was not available to me. That limited the moments I can cite to goals and shots on target, and the clip selection reflects that limit.

How many crosses a player put in is already recorded. Whether he was free when he put them in is recorded by nobody. I believe that record could become data that helps a team play better.

Open discrepancies and limits

  • The portal's on-screen shot totals (299 · 293) disagree with the event-data counts (295 · 282). The cause is unidentified, so only the event-data figures are used here.
  • From round 19 set-piece crosses are recorded under the open-play code. The set-piece codes (STCS / STCU) appear 178 times in rounds 1–18 and never after, but checking the coordinates of corner-position deliveries shows the same kicks arriving as OPCS / OPCU from round 19 on. The records were not lost; they were reclassified. A mid-season change in tagging practice seems likely, but I could not confirm the cause. In the text, crosses more than 15 seconds after the previous event are treated as dead balls and excluded (zero false positives on rounds 1–18); analysing the set pieces themselves over that stretch remains impossible.
  • Disallowed goals are erased from the event data without trace.
  • Official highlights keep only the five to ten seconds before a shot. Sequences of 29 to 56 seconds in which deliveries repeat within one attack survive in no match, so one of this piece's central findings could not be shown on video.
  • The position of any player not touching the ball is not recorded. Numbers in the box, off-ball movement and defensive shape cannot be known from this data.
  • The correlations here rest on a 25-match sample. Shot- and goal-based metrics are treated as observation rather than as a test until at least a full season is available.
