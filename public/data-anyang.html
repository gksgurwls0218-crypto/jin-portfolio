BASELINEPhase 0 · Season summary

The −10 is close to what their chances predict

Anyang have 8 wins, 11 draws and 10 defeats from 29 matches — 35 points, ninth. They have scored 36 and conceded 46. A record like that usually gets one of two explanations: bad luck, or poor finishing. The data does not strongly support either.

Expected goals for: 31.9. Expected goals against: 41.7. Expected goal difference −9.8; actual −10. A gap of 0.2 of a goal. They have scored 4.1 more than expected and conceded 4.3 more than expected — two errors of the same size, cancelling out. Finishing has not been unusually good, and the defensive phases have not been unusually bad.

The source of the deficit is equally simple. 29 of the 36 goals and 39 of the 46 concessions came in open play. The set-piece ledger is level: corners 2–2, free kicks 1–1, penalties 4–3. The goal difference was effectively created in open play.

Which points the question at the number of chances rather than at the finishing. Anyang took 110 fewer shots across the season.
COUNTER-EVIDENCEPhase 4 · Final-third output

Once they arrive, their output is the better of the two

281 shots to 391; 9.7 to 13.5 per match; 155 shots inside the box to 241. Read that far and it looks like a team that cannot create. Change the denominator and a different picture appears.

For every 100 passes played in the final third Anyang take 11.27 shots; their opponents take 10.45. Expected goals per shot also favour Anyang slightly, 0.1135 to 0.1067. While the ball is in the final third, Anyang shoot about 8% more often than the opposition.

One qualifier. Only 55% of Anyang’s shots come from inside the box, against 62% for their opponents. They shoot from slightly further out, which eats into the per-shot xG edge. "More often" is what this data supports; "from better positions" is not.
The shooting gap has more to do with time spent in the final third than with what happens once they are there.

Anyang account for 20.9% of their events in the attacking third; their opponents 26.4%. Which narrows the question to one thing — where does the ball stop?

MAPPhases 1–8

The season, laid out across eight phases

Every validated metric was re-sorted into eight phases by pitch position and possession state. "Opponents" here is not a league average but the 11 clubs Anyang actually played, in their matches against Anyang — two or three games each, so the variance is high and the direction matters more than the absolute value.

How to read it. Build-up is level with the opposition (1), output once they arrive is better (4), and losing the ball rarely leads to danger (6). The gap is concentrated in phases 2 and 3. Phase 8 (set pieces) is closer to a consequence than a cause — what is lost in 2 and 3 shows up as a set-piece count, and the aerial situation compounds it. The sections below follow that order.
THE POINTPhase 2 · Middle third (X 33–67)

The one pass out of midfield

Anyang play 5,765 passes in the middle third. 11.0% of them reach the final third; for their opponents it is 12.6%. In the other direction, 11.3% go back into their own third, against 8.5%. Slightly less forward, slightly more backward.

1.6 and 2.8 percentage points. Small, on the face of it. But Anyang play close to 200 middle-third passes a match, so it converts to three fewer forward passes and five or six more backward ones per game — roughly eight opportunities to go forward that turn around instead. And because the ball had already been advanced to the middle third, each of those turnarounds costs more than it looks.

Pass completion in the middle third is also lower, 84.8% to 88.6%. That is closer to an effect than a cause: forward passes are harder and fail more often, so a team that plays more of them should have the lower completion rate. Anyang attempt fewer forward passes and complete fewer passes overall — which makes "there are not many options to play into" a better fit for the 3.8pp than "they collapse under pressure". In their own third (phase 1), completion is 80.4% against 80.0%: identical.

The same shape appears at Gwangju FC. Through the same lens, Gwangju posted a 10.4% mid-to-final-third rate (12th in the league) and a 13.0% backward rate (2nd). Anyang, at 11.0% and 11.3%, are less extreme but pointing the same way. The location differs: at Gwangju it showed up in the defenders’ forward-pass rate (8.7%, lowest in the league); at Anyang it is the central midfielders. That is the next section.
Note: the two datasets have different cut-off dates (Gwangju 6 Sep, Anyang 13 Sep). The rate definitions were aligned, but league-rank context is not compared directly.

CAUSEPhases 2·3 · By position

Three players get into the wide attacking areas

Splitting by position locates it immediately. The share of touches taken in the wide attacking areas is 10.3% for Anyang’s defenders against 10.6% for their opponents’ — level. The gap opens among midfielders, 10.8% against 17.2%. Anyang’s midfielders appear in that zone at 63% of the opposition rate.

Three distinct groups.

  • The two full-backs get forward. Kim Dong-jin 21.9% and Lee Tae-hee 18.3%, at or above the opposition midfielder average. Not a source of the gap.
  • The three centre-backs barely enter the zone. Kwon Kyung-won 2.5%, Lee Chang-yong 1.9%, Kim Young-chan 0.7%, with mean X values of 32–33 — they stay in front of the halfway line.
  • And the three central midfielders are low as well. Kim Jung-hyun 5.1%, Thomas 8.3%, Knezevic 3.1%. The one midfielder who gets into the wide attacking areas is Matheus, at 21.6%.
Anyang’s wide attacking play is built by two full-backs and Matheus — three players.

Three can be enough. Where a front line has rehearsed patterns, output still comes with few players advancing. Which takes the next question in one direction — what happens above Matheus?

FRONT LINEFinal third · Matheus and above

There are patterns up front — but the exit tilts backward

Two hypotheses of my own, both rejected, first. The idea that the forwards do not connect with each other is not true (forward-to-forward links 1.8%, fourth in the sample). And whether their combinations repeat cannot be decided from this data — Anyang have 29 matches against two or three each for the opponents, which contaminates any "unique combination share" comparison with sample size.

The shape of the repeated combinations is clear enough. Kim Dong-jin → Matheus → Kim Dong-jin (9), Ailton → Kim Dong-jin → Ailton (7), Lee Tae-hee → Choi Gun-ju → Lee Tae-hee (6), Matheus → Kang Ji-hoon → Matheus (6). All of them give-and-goes. Wall passes between the full-backs and Matheus, and between the full-backs and the forwards, are this team’s rehearsed pattern. It is not the shape of a side without one.

And yet Matheus’s most-used outlet is Kim Jung-hyun — 27 passes, 7.4% shot conversion. The forward options are Ailton 11 (36.4%), Choi Gun-ju 13 (30.8%) and Chae Hyun-woo 10 (30.0%). The door that opens most often is the one with the lowest return.

The incoming side is balanced. The 211 passes played into him convert at 19.9% within 12 seconds, against 19.5% for the 221 played out. Going through him is not the cost. The distribution out of him tilts backward.

His 101 incomplete final-third passes are hard to read as waste. Opened up, 46% of them are crosses and set-piece deliveries — and crossing completes at 23–24% league-wide. Across the season those 101 losses produced three opposition shots and no goals. His 27.3% failure rate, on 370 attempts, sits in the middle of the squad (Vukic 48.9%, Kwon Kyung-won 41.5%, Ailton 40.0%). Team-wide, 778 final-third losses produced 8 shots against within 20 seconds (1.0%). Losing the ball in the final third costs this team almost nothing — if anything they could try more. One skew remains: of 34 losses near the box, 27 are on the right.
CONSEQUENCEPhase 8 · Set pieces and aerial presence

Set pieces do not make up the shortfall

90 corners won, 147 conceded — they concede 1.63 corners for every one they win. Crosses run at 3.14 per match, 12th in the league. That is not a stylistic choice: crosses per wide pass are 0.24 against 0.27, 89% of the opposition rate, while wide entries themselves are 34% fewer. The territorial gap from phases 2 and 3, reappearing on the flanks.

And the targets are limited when they do cross

Squad height is not the issue — it is unremarkable. Mean 181.2cm, fifth in the league; eight players at 185cm or above, five at 188cm or above. What is unusual is that the tall players are goalkeepers, defenders and midfielders.

The forwards who actually play are Choi Gun-ju 175cm (2,040 min), Kim Woon 181cm (1,491 min) and Ailton 179cm (1,313 min), with aerial-duel win rates of 33.3%, 43.9% and 41.8%. The squad as a whole wins 48.0% — it is the front line specifically that sits in the 30s and low 40s.

The players who win most first contacts at Anyang corners are Matheus 177cm (6), Choi Gun-ju 175cm (5) and Lee Chang-yong 180cm (4). Kwon Kyung-won (188cm) and Kim Young-chan (189cm) have two each. So is giving up on set pieces a reasonable choice? No. For that to hold, set pieces would have to be a net cost, and they are not: Anyang’s 90 corners produced one opposition shot within 30 seconds (1.1%), and their opponents’ 147 corners produced one Anyang shot (0.7%). Corners are close to free for this side. Finishing bottom of the league for corners is therefore not explained by counter-attack risk. Set pieces as they stand are not a loss, but they return almost nothing — few are won (territory), and the aerial conditions to use them are not in place (personnel).

Less a player who went unused than one who started and was withdrawn relatively early. He started ten matches and came off between 53 and 69 minutes in most of them. Korean reporting credits him with "189cm, 89kg and a good leap, capable of scoring plenty of headers" while noting that "judging the flight of the ball and adapting to the tempo have kept him from matching his predecessor in the air". The reason for his absence from R24 could not be established — the pattern reads like an injury, but no report confirming it was found, and the data shows only the absence.

The shape is not unique to Anyang. Gimcheon’s Jung Jae-min (192cm) was on the pitch for 31% of his own side’s corners. The same pattern appeared independently at two different clubs. The character differs — Jung was used as an impact substitute, Elcurano started and was withdrawn early.

The players who receive in the box play least

The other end matters too. Choi Gun-ju has played 2,040 minutes at 9.4 final-third touches per 90, the lowest of the regular forwards, and Kim Woon 1,491 minutes at 14.0% shot conversion, the lowest of any. Minutes and these particular rankings do not line up.

This is the weakest-evidenced section in the report. 369 and 860 minutes are small samples, and a selection effect — coming on against tiring opposition — cannot be ruled out. Read it as a hypothesis worth testing with more minutes, not as a settled conclusion.

OPENWhat is not explained

Two things left unresolved

That is as far as the data carries the argument. Two things sit outside it. Rather than force a narrative onto them, they are left as they are.

One — they concede less at home and let in more

21 points away, 14 at home. Narrowing to the 18 pairs of opponents faced both home and away, away is still +8 points, so this is not an artefact of schedule strength. The defensive numbers point the other way.

DISCIPLINEHypothesis test log

Hypotheses that did not survive

Six hypotheses raised during this analysis failed against the data. Five of them were mine. They are kept rather than deleted — a rejected hypothesis draws the boundary around the conclusions that survived, and stops the same mistake recurring.

✕

Losses in midfield are the source of the 391 shots faced

Rejected. Tracing each of the 391 opposition shots back 25 seconds to the last change of possession, 48 (12.3%) began with an Anyang misplaced pass, and only 8 (2%) with one in the central zone. This data does not support a counter-attack narrative. The real gap was sustained-possession shots, 66 to 24, which is what led into the territory argument.

✕

Using few set pieces is a rational way to avoid counter-attacks

Rejected. Anyang’s 90 corners produced one opposition shot within 30 seconds (1.1%). Corners are close to free for this side. There is no basis for treating avoidance as a rational choice.

✕

They are structurally worse at defending at home

Partly rejected. The points reversal survives controlling for opposition, but home is lower on both shots faced and xGA. Removing one 0–7 takes home concessions from 1.86 to 1.46. Closer to "fewer chances conceded, more goals conceded at home" than to a defensive collapse.

✕

The forwards do not connect with each other

Rejected. None of the top 14 final-third combination pairs was forward-to-forward, and I read that as an absence. But top-N lists skew toward players with the most minutes. Recounted at club level, forward-to-forward links are 1.8%, fourth in the sample. Absence from a top-N list is not absence.

✕

Their three-player combinations repeat more than any side in the league (= there are rehearsed patterns)

Downgraded to undecidable. The basis — "unique/total combinations 0.78, lowest in the league" — was sample-size bias: 29 matches for Anyang against two or three for each opponent. Subsampling Anyang to three matches 2,000 times lifts the median to 0.919 [0.868, 0.975], and a matched within-match comparison gives Anyang 0.914 against 0.879 — less repetitive, if anything — at p=0.508. Whether the patterns exist cannot be decided from this data.

✕

Matheus’s 101 incomplete final-third passes are waste

Rejected. This was an item I had proposed as worth a closer look, and it was not. His failure rate is mid-squad, 46% of the losses are crosses and set-piece deliveries, and the 101 produced three opposition shots and no goals.

Three methodological traps the re-validation exposed. A full re-validation was run before drafting, and two figures were corrected before publication. ① Do not convert a live-event half-minute with a fixed +45 — actual first-half endings range from 46 to 57 minutes. This is what led me to read Elcurano’s usage as "21 appearances, all from the bench" (actually 18 appearances, 10 starts, 8 substitutions). ② Do not compare "unique" ratios across groups with different observation counts — the fifth item above. ③ Do not read absence from a top-N list as absence — the fourth item above. Separately, "Matheus takes all the corners" was also wrong — 26 of 90 (29%), with Ailton 16, Lee Tae-hee 13, Vukic 12 and Kim Dong-jin 10.
ACTIONSPriority order

Where to start, and what to leave alone

The gap sits in phases 2 and 3, so the interventions start there. They are ordered by cost — not recruitment or a change of shape, but the positions and choices of players already in the squad.

1

Let one central midfielder advance

No central midfielder other than Matheus exceeds a 10% share of touches in the wide attacking areas (Kim Jung-hyun 5.1%, Thomas 8.3%, Knezevic 3.1%). The opposition midfielder average is 17.2%.

It is the most direct lever on the 11.0% forward rate in phase 2, and the cheapest thing in this squad to change. The residual risk behind the ball is far smaller than pushing a centre-back forward.

Evidence · wide attacking touches 10.8% vs 17.2% · defenders level at 10.3% vs 10.6%
2

Move Matheus’s first option forward

His most-used outlet today is Kim Jung-hyun (27 passes, 7.4% shot conversion). The three forward options convert at 30–36% but received only 10–13 passes each.

This is workable on the training ground. It changes neither the personnel nor the system — only where he looks first when he receives in the final third.

Evidence · forward 32.4% vs backward 6.6% · z=3.31 · p=0.0009 — the most robust individual finding in this report
3

Reduce the long-pass share of final-third entries

Long passes make up 45% of Anyang’s entry attempts against 41% for their opponents, while the long-pass success rate is 40% for both. Same success rate, higher share, lower expected return.

Entries are also more central, 20% against 14% — the narrowest lane in.

Evidence · entry success 56.6% vs 62.9% · long-pass success identical at 40%
4

Give corner deliveries a target

31% first contact means the delivery is not reaching the tall players. Kwon Kyung-won (188cm) and Kim Young-chan (189cm) have won two first contacts each; the leader is Matheus at 177cm with six.

Since corners themselves are close to free (1.1% shots faced within 30 seconds), committing more bodies is also cheap.

Evidence · first contact 31% vs 62% · identical at 6, 10 and 15s windows · mean height at corners 181.6cm
5

Give the box-reception profiles more minutes, and test it

Blaise (16.4% box receptions, 369 min) and Elcurano (15.3%, 860 min) take more final-third touches per 90 than the regulars. Choi Gun-ju is at 9.4 per 90 across 2,040 minutes.

This is a proposed test, not a conclusion. The sample is small and a selection effect has not been ruled out. Giving them minutes is itself the test.

Evidence · box receptions 16.4% and 15.3% (1st and 2nd in the squad) · but 369 and 860 minutes — treat with care

Better left alone

✕

The build-up structure

Phase 1 is level with the opposition (pass completion 80.4% vs 80.0%, own-third exit rate 22.6% vs 22.8%, long-ball share 20.7% vs 19.8%). There is no basis for saying it is breaking under pressure.

✕

Final-third execution

Phase 4 already favours Anyang (11.27 shots per 100 passes against 10.45; 0.1135 xG per shot against 0.1067). Little to gain from touching an area that is already ahead.

✕

Suppressing turnovers

In phase 6, 30 of 2,421 losses led to a shot within 15 seconds (1.2%); in the final third, 8 of 778 within 20 seconds (1.0%). Asking for "safety" would push an already low 11.0% forward rate lower still.

✕

Pushing the centre-backs forward

Filling the wide shortfall from the back. Goals conceded are already second-highest in the league, and concessions while leading match expectation (1.75 vs 1.76). There is no case for emptying more space behind. The gap forms in front of the defensive line, not in it.
