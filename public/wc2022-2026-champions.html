Chapter 01 · Method

The two tournaments cannot be measured with the same ruler

One problem has to be settled before anything else. Put FIFA's two reports side by side and compare the numbers, and you end up reading a change in FIFA's ruler as a change in football.

Forced turnovers fell from an average of 71.1 per match to 41.5 — a 42% drop. Ball-winning cannot have nearly halved in 4 years. The quartile ranges of the two tournaments do not even overlap (2022: 38–104; 2026: 23–59). This is a change of definition.

So all 35 metrics were audited on three criteria — change in mean · change in standard deviation · quartile overlap — and sorted into three grades.

Figure 1 — Metric-definition audit

Bar length = the % change from the 2022 mean to the 2026 mean. Bar colour carries the grade — this is the only figure in the report that uses the grade colours. Metric names are exactly as they appear in the official FIFA reports (hover a bar for a plain-language description).

Stable (22) — raw comparison is fine Caution (5) — compare by z-score only Definition change suspected (8) — no raw comparison
Zone 4 distance (−62%) carries a different label altogether — in 2022 it is High Speed (Zone 4) Distance Covered, in 2026 Zone 4 – Low Speed Sprinting: 20-25 km/h. Even inside 2022 the label differs between matches 1–24 and matches from 25 onwards. It is excluded from this report entirely.
Solution

Tournament-relative z-score

Each metric is converted to a z-score within the distribution of all team-matches in its own tournament, and the resulting z-profiles are what get compared.

z(team, metric) = ( team per-match mean − field mean of that tournament ) ÷ field standard deviation of that tournament

This single conversion solves three problems at once. Definition changes → the field moves with them, so they cancel out 32 teams→48 teams dilution → dividing by the width of the distribution normalises it and the scale now matches the question itself: "how does a side differ within its tournament's trend?"

And there is one more thing — 2022 has no xG

All 64 of the 2022 PMSRs were checked. The line Expected Goals appears not once. FIFA did not carry xG in the official reports for the 2022 tournament.

Without xG you cannot measure who created the better chances. Shot counts alone are not enough — a shot struck 1v1 with the goalkeeper inside the box and a hopeful effort from 30 metres out both count as 1.

Filling the gap with third-party xG data was an option, but it was not used. Every provider calculates xG differently. Set provider A's 2022 xG next to FIFA's 2026 xG and you can no longer tell a tournament difference from a provider difference. So a different route was taken.

Method

Learn "how to guess xG" from 2026, then apply that method to 2022

2026 has xG, and it has the other metrics too. So a computer can be asked to find "the rule that predicts xG from the other metrics". If that rule is accurate enough, the same rule applied to 2022 gives "the value xG would have shown". This is what is called the Threat Index.

Step 1 · Picking the ingredients — this is as far as the human went

You cannot just throw any metric at the computer. Three conditions did the filtering.

Condition one — it must exist in both tournaments. A metric missing from 2022 cannot be applied to 2022.

Condition two — it must be graded "stable" in Figure 1. If FIFA changed the way it counts something, applying it to 2022 returns nonsense. This condition is why receptions in the final third were dropped — an excellent measure of threat in principle, but its maximum jumps from 174 in 2022 to 426 in 2026, which points to a change of definition.

Condition three — they must not overlap with each other. This is where the first attempt went wrong. It is explained in full below.

One number to know first

R² — how well the prediction landed

It keeps coming up from here on. It runs from 0 to 1, and the closer to 1, the better the fit. What matters is knowing exactly what it measures.

Think of a game where you guess your friends' heights. With no information at all, the best you can do is answer "170cm, the average" every time. That is the baseline. Now let someone tell you their shoe size and you will do far better.

R² measures "by what % did you cut the error, compared with just guessing the average". R² = 0 means you did no better than guessing the average; 1 means perfect. Bigger is better.

In football data, anything in the 0.6s is fairly high. It is a sport played by people, and the remainder is territory that was never predictable in the first place.

Why the first model was discarded

The crosses coefficient came out negative — a shape that cannot be defended in football terms

The first model used six ingredients: shots on target · total shots · final-third phase share · crosses · completed line breaks · defensive line breaks. Accuracy was good at R² 0.687, but the crosses coefficient came out at −0.0236. That reads as "the more crosses you put in, the less threat you create".

This is not football, it is a statistical side effect. Take crosses on their own against xG and the correlation is r = +0.404 — clearly positive. Sides that crossed more also had higher xG.

The problem was that the ingredients overlapped badly. Total shots and shots on target correlate at 0.78; crosses and final-third phase share correlate at 0.78. In effect the same thing was being counted twice, or three times. In that situation a regression fights over the shared portion and shoves one coefficient into the negative (multicollinearity). It does not mean crosses subtract threat; it means the model adjusted so that crosses would not re-count the share the shots had already taken.

Statistically that is explicable, but you cannot use a metric that collides with the football common sense that "a cross is an active attacking act that delivers the ball into a dangerous area". So the ingredients were redesigned.

Redesign — three things changed

What changedWhy
Total shots droppedCorrelates 0.78 with shots on target. It counted the same thing twice and was the main culprit in the fight over coefficients
Crosses → completed crossesCounts only the deliveries that actually reached a team-mate. The correlation with xG also rises from 0.404 to 0.474
Every coefficient fixed at 0 or aboveThe principle that "attacking acts do not subtract threat" is nailed into the calculation rule itself. The computer cannot use a negative even if it wants to

The third one is the key. When you are confident a principle holds in football, constraining the data in advance so it cannot break that principle is the honest approach. In statistical terms it is called non-negative least squares.

Step 2 · Setting the weights — from here the computer did the work

How much each of the five ingredients should count was not decided by a human. The computer looked at all 208 team-matches from 2026 and found the combination with the smallest error against actual xG. Under one constraint: every coefficient must be 0 or above.

By analogy: you know the weight, height, age and shoe size of 208 people and you are filling in the question marks in "weight = ?×height + ?×age + ?×shoe size", with one extra rule that "a question mark cannot be negative". The result looks like this.

Threat Index  =  −0.154 (starting value)
           + 0.1716 × shots on target
           + 0.0136 × final-third phase share (%)
           + 0.0111 × completed crosses
           + 0.0110 × defensive line breaks
           + 0.0036 × completed line breaks
All five are positive. And not one ingredient died at 0 — all five pull their weight.

How to read the numbers. The 0.1716 in front of shots on target means "1 more shot on target adds 0.17 of xG". 6 shots on target gives 0.17 × 6 ≈ 1.0 xG. That matches the feel of anyone who knows the game.

What the coefficients revealed

Breaking the last line weighs 3 times as much as simply getting past a line

Defensive line breaks 0.0110 against completed line breaks 0.0036. A factor of 3. The data says that putting the ball behind the last defensive line is far more threatening than slipping a pass between lines in the opposition half. No human set that; it is the output of a calculation across 208 matches.

Completed crosses weigh almost exactly the same as defensive line breaks

Completed crosses 0.0111, defensive line breaks 0.0110. Effectively identical. It means connecting a ball from wide into the box is worth the same as breaking the last line through the middle. That is the opposite conclusion to the first model, which treated crosses as a deduction — and it agrees with football common sense.

When it is accurate

The bigger the gap the better it does; the tighter the match the worse

Across all 104 matches, the rate at which "which of the two sides was on top" was called correctly is 77.9%. But that rate splits sharply by type of match.

That is actually a reassuring result. What this report deals in is the large difference between sides that created threat and sides that did not, not tight matches decided in the second decimal place. Splitting into 4 quartiles and comparing top with bottom is precisely the way to look at that large difference.

Why cross-validation is needed. The computer built the formula by looking at those 208 cases, so of course it fits those 208 well. It is like writing your answers after seeing the exam paper.

So this was done instead. The 208 cases were split into five pieces; the computer is shown only four pieces to build the formula, then asked to predict the piece it was not shown. Rotate the pieces and repeat five times. The result, 0.591, barely drops from the original 0.615 — which means it learned a real rule rather than memorising.

Adopted

The threshold was set first, and it passed, so it is used

The threshold set before any calculation began was "discard it if R² is under 0.55 or cross-validated R² is under 0.50". The result, 0.615 / 0.591, passed, so it is used. Had it fallen short, the index would have been thrown away and shots on target alone would have carried the work — and that failure would have been written up right here too.

And one thing to hold on to alongside it — this is not xG, it is an approximation of xG. Winner accuracy is 5.0 percentage points below actual xG. Chapter 11 checks which way this approximation distorts the conclusions (the short answer: it comes out more conservative than reality).

Chapter 02 · The 2022 trend

2022 — the tournament of the mid-block

Anyone who watched Qatar 2022 shares an impression. Nobody pressed furiously high up the pitch, and nobody sat huddled in front of their own goal either. Everyone stood in the middle. That impression survives intact in the data.

Split the out-of-possession phases into high block, mid-block and low block, and calculate each side's share within those three, and this is what comes out.

Low block40.8%46.8%+6.0%p

In 2022 the mid-block is 48.9% and the low block 40.8%. In 2026 that reverses: mid-block 43.1%, low block 46.8%. The high-block share sits near 10% in both tournaments, essentially unchanged — what changed was not "how high you press" but "whether you stand in the middle or drop off."

But the real finding is the relationship with results

A high share is only a trend in itself. The question is what happened to the sides that followed it. Sides were split into thirds by their tournament-average mid-block share, and the mean rounds reached was calculated for each group.

Figure 3 — mid-block share and rounds reached key

Rounds reached: 0 = group-stage exit … 5 = reached the final. 2022 is a perfectly monotone staircase; in 2026 the staircase disappears

2022: top third 2.40 → middle third 1.27 → bottom third 0.73. 2026: 1.19 → 1.12 → 1.56 (if anything, a weak reversal). This staircase was calculated within each tournament only, so it is unaffected by the expansion to 48 sides.

Splitting sides into those whose mid-block share exceeds their low-block share and those where the reverse holds gives the same picture. In 2022 the 22 mid-block-dominant sides reached a mean of 1.77 rounds, against 0.70 for the 10 low-block-dominant sides. In 2026 it is 1.53 against 1.14 — the gap shrinks to less than half.

In the 2022 semi-finals all four sides spent more time in a mid-block than in a low block. In 2026 half of them are flipped. The FIFA Technical Study Group (TSG) reached the same conclusion — "all four semi-finalists used the mid-block as their principal defensive phase", "sides holding 33% possession or less recorded 14 wins and draws against 5 defeats."

Endogeneity warning

Mid-block share is both a cause and a symptom

Whether sides could stand in the middle because they were strong, or became strong by standing in the middle, cannot be separated with this data. Weak sides are pushed into a low block from the start. The staircase in Figure 3 must not be read as "use a mid-block and you reach the semi-finals."

One thing is certain, though — that staircase existed in 2022 and does not exist in 2026. If the same endogeneity operates in both tournaments and the staircase appears in only one of them, the difference comes from the character of the tournament, not from team strength.

Chapter 03 · How sides lost in 2022

In 2022 the side that lost was the side that ran more

To explain why the mid-block won, we have to look at the other side of it. The opposite of the mid-block is not the low block but excess. What happened to the sides that ran more and pressed more.

Here the hypothesis splits in two. "Running a lot" and "pressing a lot" are usually talked about as one thing, but in the data they behave completely differently.

Two biases to settle first

Without adjusting for matches played and extra time, the answer comes out distorted

Bias one — matches played. A side that reaches the final plays 7-8 matches; a side that goes out in the group stage plays only 3 of them. Counting "which sides turn up in this band" means the sides that survived longest automatically appear more often. That is not evidence of quality, it is arithmetic.

Bias two — extra time. 5 matches in 2022 and 9 in 2026 went to extra time. Team distance covered in those matches averaged 145km, more than 30% above matches decided in normal time (112km). They ran 30 minutes longer, so that is only to be expected.

So two things were done. ① Extra-time matches were converted to a 90-minute basis (120 minutes × 3/4), and ② the unit is the team, not the team-match, with that side's per-match average as the figure. This way every side is counted exactly once.

Figure 4 — Distance covered per match and rounds reached (extra-time adjusted)

Every side in each tournament was ranked by distance covered per match, converted to 90 minutes, and split into 4 quartiles. Q1 is the sides that ran least, Q4 the sides that ran most. The vertical axis is that group's mean rounds reached — higher means further (0 = group-stage exit, 5 = the final)

In 2022 the half that ran less overwhelms the rest. The 16 sides in the lower half of distance covered have a mean of 2.00 rounds reached, the 16 in the upper half 0.88. That is a 2.3-fold gap.

More decisive is the team list below. All 4 of the 2022 semi-finalists sit in Q1 and Q2, that is, in the half that ran less. Q1 has Argentina and France; Q2 has Morocco and Croatia. Q3 and Q4 hold none of the 4 semi-finalists.

2026 is different. The 4 semi-finalists are scattered across Q1·Q2·Q3 — and the gap is far narrower, 1.46 for the lower half against 1.12 for the upper.

Checking it directly, head to head

Comparing the 4 quartiles mixes side strength together. The cleanest test is comparing the two sides inside the same match. Of two sides that ran under identical conditions for 90 minutes (or 120), did the one that ran less win?

Figure 5 — Distance head-to-head across the 16 knockout matches of 2022 key

Each line is one match. Left is the side that ran less, right the side that ran more. Bold line = the side that ran less won

Side that ran less won (9) Side that ran more won (2) Penalty shoot-out (5)
In the 11 matches that produced a winner, the side that ran less won 9 (81.8%). The 2 matches won by the side that ran more were England 3-0 Senegal and Portugal 6-1 Switzerland — both routs. Sides ran more only when they were overwhelming the opponent.

The tight rounds admit no exceptions. France ran less than the opponent and won 4 matches in a row — 106.4km to 107.8km against Poland (3-1), 109.2km to 112.8km against England (2-1), 106.9km to 112.5km against Morocco (2-0). In the semi-final Argentina won 3-0 while running 200m less than Croatia.

Apply the same calculation to the 2026 knockouts and it is 12 of the 28 matches that produced a winner (42.9%) — a coin toss. The effect disappeared completely in 4 years.

Pressing, though, goes the other way

This is where the received wisdom breaks. The sentence "the sides that ran a lot and pressed a lot lost" sounds like a description of 2022 — but where pressing is concerned, 2022 shows no relationship at all.

Figure 6 — Pressures per match and rounds reached (extra-time adjusted)

The same method as Figure 4 above. Sides were split into 4 quartiles by pressures per match, converted to 90 minutes. Q1 is the sides that pressed least, Q4 the sides that pressed most

In 2022 the volume of pressing barely separated results. The lower-pressing half sits at 1.69, the upper-pressing half at 1.19. And the 4 semi-finalists are scattered across Q1·Q2·Q4 — Morocco made the last 4 while sitting in the group that pressed most in the tournament.

2026 is another matter entirely. 1.92 for the half that pressed less, 0.67 for the half that pressed more. Almost a 3-fold gap. And all 4 semi-finalists are in Q1 and Q2 — Argentina in Q1, Spain, France and England in Q2 — Q3 and Q4 hold none of the 4 semi-finalists.

Q4 holds 12 sides: Australia, Czechia, Cabo Verde, Ghana, Iran, Austria, DR Congo, Uzbekistan, Paraguay, Curaçao, Saudi Arabia and Jordan. Mean rounds reached 0.58 — most of them went out in the group stage.

Corrected claim

The cause of defeat moved from total volume to distribution

In 2022 the volume of pressing did not separate winners from losers. It was random, somewhere between 34% and 44%. In 2026 the steepest slope in this entire dataset appears — from 55.8% in the least-pressing of the 4 quartiles down to 11.5% in the most-pressing 4th.

In other words, the 2022 lesson was "run less" and the 2026 lesson is "press less and win it back more". They point the same way, but the layer being measured is different. In 2022 the question was still "how much do you run"; by 2026 it had become "what do you spend that running on".

— But whether "run less" is a prescription or a by-product cannot be settled from this section alone. The first check in Chapter 11 answers that question.

Hypothesis check — "the sides that ran a lot and pressed a lot underperformed"

Half supported. The "running a lot" half is strongly confirmed in 2022 (81.8% in the knockouts); the "pressing" half is disconfirmed in 2022 and confirmed in 2026 — the original hypothesis had folded the phenomena of two tournaments into one.

Chapter 04 · The two 2022 finalists

Argentina and France inside it — two sides that climbed by giving up possession

Now that the character of the tournament is known, the question narrows. Within that trend, how did the two sides that reached the final differ?

Start with the point where Argentina stood out from the tournament. Convert each of the 16 metrics to a tournament-relative z-score and only one axis rises clearly.

Figure 7 — z-profiles of the 4 finalists the heart of this report

How many standard deviations each side sits from the mean of its own tournament. 0 = tournament mean. The checkboxes switch sides on and off

The single metric on which Argentina 2022 deviated most from the field is shots on target (+1.10). Everything else is "evenly good", in the +0.5 to +0.9 range. Where Spain 2026 stood out is structural throughout — completed line breaks +1.97, high press +1.50, forced turnovers +1.10. Yet their shots on target figure of +0.86 is lower than Argentina's.

Argentina 2022 were not a side that overwhelmed the tournament on structural metrics. Line breaks +0.73, possession +0.65, final-third phase share +0.65 — all of it "decent enough". The one place where they were clearly ahead of the field was shots on target. It is the profile of an ordinarily good side that spiked at the finish.

France are another matter again. The metric on which France deviated most is mid-block share (+1.06). Shots on target is only +0.39 by comparison. France were the side that executed the tournament's dominant trend most faithfully.

And the two sides traced the same curve

Follow possession in match order and the same pattern comes out of both finalists.

Pattern

Both sides put the ball down as the rounds went up

Argentina came down from 67.4% against Poland to 34.4% against Croatia, and that 34.4% match was a 3-0 win. France came down from 56.4% against Tunisia (a defeat) to 34.1% against Morocco, and that match was a 2-0 win.

For both, the match with the lowest possession was the match they won most convincingly. And both lost matches in which they had more of the ball — Argentina lost to Saudi Arabia with 62%, France lost to Tunisia with 56.4%.

This is not coincidence but the logic of a mid-block tournament. When the opponent stands in the middle, the ball comes easily but no threat travels with it. In 2022 having the ball was not an advantage but a task.

Argentina reached the final in three shapes

The formations tell the same story. Argentina 2022 moved between 4-4-2 · 4-1-2-3 · 3-5-2 across 7 matches (1 match not recorded in the FIFA original), and France alternated between the two shapes 4-1-2-3 ↔ 4-2-3-1.

Yet 4 years later Spain played 7 of their 8 matches in a single 4-1-2-3 and won it. Conversely, Argentina 2026 used four different shapes and finished as runners-up.

The side that changed most won, and the side that changed least won. Formation variation is a cheap way of keeping the opponent from knowing what to prepare for the next match; it is not a condition of winning. That is why this item is recorded as disconfirmed in the Chapter 9 scoring.

Chapter 05 · The 2026 trend

2026 — the tournament of the low block, and the price of pressing

The tournament, expanded to 48 sides, wore a different face. The mid-block staircase had gone, and another staircase appeared in its place.

The low-block share in 2026 is 23.2%, higher than the 19.1% of 2022. But that figure has the 48-side expansion effect mixed into it and must not be read at face value (metric-definition audit grade). What is certain is the relationship within a tournament.

And then there is pressing. Looking again at Figure 6 from Chapter 3, the 2026 pressing-quartile staircase (55.8 → 55.8 → 30.8 → 11.5%) is the steepest gradient in this entire dataset. Converted to pressing efficiency, it falls from 59.6% to 9.6%.

The character of the two tournaments

2022 was the more random tournament

Comparing win rates by Threat Index quartile across the two tournaments produces an interesting difference.

2022: 21.9 → 34.4 → 40.6 → 56.2%  ·  2026: 7.7 → 30.8 → 46.2 → 69.2%

In 2026 the group that generated the most threat wins 69.2% of the time. In 2022 the same group stops at 56.2%. The other end behaves the same way: the group that generated the least threat won 21.9% in 2022 but only 7.7% in 2026.

In 2022 it was hard to win even when you created good chances, and possible to survive even when you did not. The Threat Index also picks the winner 69.4% of the time in 2022 against 81.2% in 2026. A tournament of the mid-block turns out to mean a tournament with few decisive moments at either end.

Chapter 07 · The individual

The star walks

Everything up to here has been about sides. The last layer is the individual. The FIFA PMSR carries, for each player, pressures (direct and indirect) and distance covered by speed zone (Zone 1–5). All 5,287 player-matches from the two tournaments were pulled.

Zone 1 is 0–7 km/h — in practice, walking. Calculate walking as a share of total distance covered and you see exactly how a player budgeted his 90 minutes.

In the 2022 final Messi covered 12,109m in total. Of that, 7,128m was walking. In the same match Enzo Fernández walked only 4,905m of his 15,055m.

Among the 2022 field of 100 outfield players, 1st in walking share is Messi (57.9%) and 2nd is Mbappé (51.7%) — the stars of the two sides that reached the final. 3rd to 6th are all centre-backs (Romero, Thiago Silva, Glik, Van Dijk). Chapter 09 · Scoring

Has my philosophy become more valid over four years

I write down where the data supported my thinking and where it contradicted it, together. Drop the items that were contradicted and this report stops being a check and becomes promotion.

SUPPORTED

Defending is structure, not effort — and it has grown more defensible with time

In 2022 the amount of pressing had nothing to do with results: the 4 quartiles ran 34–44%. In 2026 the least-pressing quartile won 55.8% and the most-pressing quartile 11.5%, the steepest slope across the 4 quartiles anywhere in this dataset. Both champions pressed less than their own tournament mean (−0.60, −0.27), and the champions' forced-turnover z flipped from −0.03 to +1.10 between the two tournaments. Over 4 years this claim became stronger.

And this claim is the only conclusion in this report that passes the team-strength control — compared within the same side, 23 of 32 sides took more points in their "less pressing" matches, with a mean points-per-match gap of +0.82 (Chapter 11). Running volume did not pass.

CONDITIONAL

The individual variable — valid, conditionally

The 2022 champion stood out on a single axis, shots on target (+1.10); the 2026 champion stood out on structural metrics (its shots-on-target z is in fact lower). A title that leans on individual moments is observed in 2022; in 2026 it weakens. But a new piece of evidence appeared, walking share — in all 4 cases of a side reaching the final, the star ranked at the top of the tournament for walking. To run the individual variable you have to bank the energy for it: for the first time that is confirmed with physical data.

SUPPORTED

Proactivity — valid, but the axis is not possession

Win rate rises monotonically across the 4 Threat Index quartiles in both tournaments (2022 25→56.2%, 2026 11.5→71.2%). Yet the two sides that reached the 2022 final won most convincingly in the matches where they had the least of the ball (Argentina beat Croatia 3-0, on 34.4% possession, and France beat Morocco 2-0 on 34.1%). Proactivity is not having the ball, it is creating threat.

DISCONFIRMED

Formation variation — not a necessary condition

Argentina 2022 won with 3 shapes, and Spain 2026 won with what was effectively 1 shape (4-1-2-3 in 7 of 8 matches). Argentina 2026 used 4 shapes and finished runner-up. The side that changed most won, and the side that changed least won. Variation is a cheap way to leave an opponent unsure what to prepare for; it is not a condition of winning.

UNRESOLVED

Multi-positionality — there is still no way to measure it

The attempt to use defenders' share of ball progression as a proxy for role fluidity failed in the 2026 analysis (the top of the 4 quartiles won 23.1%). That metric was measuring not role fluidity but a side whose progression is blocked. This time again I found no alternative. It stays unresolved.

Chapter 10 · Limits

Limits — what this report could not answer

1. Endogeneity — cause and symptom were never separated

Mid-block share, low-block share, distance covered and the star player's pressing volume are all symptoms of a side's strength as much as anything else. Weak sides sit deep, sides being pushed back run more, and only strong sides can afford to spare their star. The staircase in Figure 3 must not be read as causation.

The nearest thing to a defence in this report is the head-to-head within the same match (Figure 6) and the 4-year comparison within the same country (Chapter 8). Both hold side strength partly constant. Even so, it is not a clean separation.

2. Sample size

The 81.8% in the 2022 knockouts rests on the 11 matches that were decided. It leaves out the 5 draws (penalty shoot-outs), and n is small. Only 2 sides reach the final per tournament, so the figure "4/4" is 4 cases. It makes a strong impression, but the statistical power is low.

3. Metric-definition drift

8 of the 35 metrics are graded definition change suspected (forced turnovers, total offers to receive, take-ons, step-ins, receptions in the final third, share of offers in behind, transition passes, Zone 4 running). Zone 4 was dropped entirely, and the rest were used as z-scores only. Even so, any sentence in which these metrics appear is less reliable than the sentences around it.

4. The xG substitute

The Threat Index is an approximation with an R² of 0.615 behind it. Its winner hit rate of 81.2% is 5.0pp below real xG's 86.2%. Every conclusion computed with this index could come out slightly differently with real xG. Check 5 in Chapter 11 establishes the direction of that distortion — it comes out more conservative than reality.

5. There is no time axis

This is the most important limit. PMSR is match-total data, so the time structure inside a sequence is invisible. There is no way to know how many seconds apart the line breaks happened, or how long it took to reach the final third after the defensive line was broken. Nor how many repetitions it took before Lure and Shock worked.

Next step: from event-level logs, measure (a) the standard deviation of the interval between line breaks and (b) the time from a defensive line break to entry into the final third. That is the point at which the analogy becomes a number.

6. The direction of causation — Chapter 7 above all

Whether the star not defending is what took the side to the final, or whether being a side bound for the final is what made sparing the star affordable, cannot be separated with this data. Chapter 7 is the observation "the sides that reached the final were doing it this way", not a prescription.

Chapter 11 · Checks

Checks — how robust are these conclusions

Chapter 10 listed the limits. Listing them is not enough. Here each conclusion is actually run through a check. And one of them did not pass.

Check 1 — what happens when side strength is held constant

The biggest risk in this report is endogeneity. If weak sides run a lot because they are being pushed back, and press a lot because they are being pushed back, then "the sides that ran more lost" is just another way of saying "the weak sides lost".

The cleanest way to separate the two is to compare within the same side. Split one side's matches into halves by that side's own running volume (or pressing volume), and compare points per match in the half where it did less against the half where it did more. Side strength becomes a constant by design.

Figure 11 — within-team dumbbells the most important check

Sides with 4 or more matches only. Each line is one side. Left dot = mean points from the matches where that side did less, right dot = mean points from the matches where it did more

One conclusion did not pass its check

"The sides that lost in 2022 are the sides that ran more" — this does not hold within a side

On 2022 distance covered, of the 16 sides that played 4 or more matches, only 4 took more points in "the matches where that side ran less". 10 were the other way round, and the mean points-per-match gap is −0.30. Within the same side, that is, results were actually better in the matches where they ran more.

This collides head-on with the between-side comparison in Chapter 3 (Figures 4 and 5). Put the two results together and the reading narrows to this — "the sides that ran less won", not "running less makes you win". The sides that did well in 2022 were structurally low-running sides (mid-block sides), and those sides won. But a side running less on a given day did not win more that day.

The sentence in Chapter 3 is corrected: not "the sides that ran more lost" but "the sides that won in 2022 were the low-running kind of side" is the accurate version. Running volume is most likely not a cause but a by-product of a cause called the mid-block. And that reading meshes exactly with the Chapter 2 mid-block staircase.

Passes

"The sides that lost in 2026 are the sides that pressed more" — this survives the within-side comparison too

Run the same calculation on 2026 pressing volume and 23 of 32 sides took more points in "the matches where they pressed less", with a mean points-per-match gap of +0.82. A gap of 0.82 points per match is large.

It is the only claim whose direction holds when side strength is held constant. The conclusion in this report that comes closest to causation is pressing. Not running volume.

Check 2 — answering the charge that the sample is small

The 2022 knockout figure of 81.8% rests on 11 matches. Whether that number could be chance was checked two ways.

Bootstrap — put the 11 results you have into a bag, draw one out, write it down, put it back, and repeat 11 times to build a fake tournament. Do that 20,000 times. What you get is a picture of "if this tournament were played again 20,000 times, roughly where would the result land".

TournamentMatchesLower-running side wins95% confidence intervalProbability of chanceVerdict
2022 knockouts1181.8%[54.5%, 100%]p = 0.033not chance
2026 knockouts2842.9%[25.0%, 60.7%]—no relationship

The confidence interval is the range that holds the middle 95% of those 20,000 results. 2022 comes out at [54.5%, 100%], which does not contain 50% — not chance. 2026 comes out at [25.0%, 60.7%], which does contain 50% — meaning there is no relationship.

p = 0.033 means that "the probability of getting a result this strong purely by luck, when there is no relationship at all" is 3.3%. Below 5% is normally taken as not chance.

That said, a lower bound of 54.5% also means it cannot tell "almost always" apart from "slightly favoured". The honest wording is this — that the side which ran less was favoured in the 2022 knockouts is confirmed, but the size of that edge cannot be measured precisely with this sample.

Check 3 — is the mid-block staircase chance

The staircase in Figure 3 is what came out of splitting 32 sides into 3 groups. With only 32 sides, that shape could appear by chance.

It was checked with a permutation test. Shuffle the sides' rounds reached as if drawing lots, stick them onto sides at random, then compute the correlation. Repeat that 20,000 times and you build "the pile of values you get when there is no relationship at all". If the actual observed value sits outside that pile, it is not chance.

Chapter 12 · Conclusion

So what is left

Eleven chapters, reduced to one. Mix conclusions of different confidence into a single sentence and the weakest eats away at the credibility of the strongest. So how confident I am is attached first, and they are written out as three separate things.

The three that are left

Most solid · closest to causation

① In 2026 pressing was not a measure of intensity but a measure of waste

The least-pressing quartile won 55.8% of its matches. The most-pressing quartile won 11.5%. The steepest gradient in the whole dataset. All 4 semi-finalists are in Q1 and Q2 — Q3 and Q4 hold none of them.

And this is the only conclusion that passes all three layers of checks — compared within the same side, 23 of 32 teams took more points in the matches where they pressed less (mean +0.82), and if forced turnovers, whose definition changed, are removed entirely and the sum is run on pressures alone, the staircase still comes out. It is not explained by team strength, and it is not explained by FIFA changing its ruler.

Solid, but correlational · not a prescription

② 2022 was a mid-block tournament, and the sides that won it were the kind of side that runs less

Mean rounds reached for the top 1/3 in mid-block 2.40 against 0.73 for the bottom 1/3 (permutation test p=0.011, 2·3·4·5-way splits all in the same direction). All 4 of the last 4 were mid-block dominant. In the knockouts the side that ran less won 9 of the 11 matches that were decided (81.8%).

But within the same side the direction reverses (mean points-per-match gap −0.30). So the accurate sentence is not "run less and you win" but "the sides that won in 2022 were structurally the kind of side that runs less — that is, mid-block sides". Distance covered is not the cause; it is a by-product of a cause called the mid-block.

Observation · n=4, endogeneity cannot be controlled

③ In all four cases that reached a final, the star was near the top of the tournament for walking

Messi (57.9%, 1st) · Mbappé (51.7%, 2nd) · Yamal (48.4%, 13th) · Messi (64.7%, 1st). But the correlation at team level is r = −0.08, effectively 0 — and many of the sides that took their star out of defensive work went out in the group stage. It is not a condition for reaching the last 4 but a condition observed only at the summit.

Sharper than that is the line that divided the last 4 itself. All four sides funnelled the attack through one player; that was the same. What divided them was whether they lightened his defensive work in exchange — Mbappé 0.67× · Messi 0.84× beat Perišić 0.97× · En-Nesyri 1.40×, 2-0, 3-0 respectively.

What did not change completes the conclusion

All three conclusions speak to "what changed". Yet the most striking number in this report is on the side that did not change. Different tournaments, 48 different teams, measurements taken 4 years apart — and the position of the defensive block is almost completely still.

And both pressed less than the mean of their own tournament (−0.60, −0.27).

Three sentences for the training ground

That is what the data said; below is the shape it takes when applied to a side. ① can be used as a prescription; ② and ③ are material for judgement only.

1. Manage pressing by allocation, not by volume

The moment total pressing becomes a training target, you enter 2026's Q4 (win rate 11.5%). The number to manage is not pressures but pressures ÷ forced turnovers — how many times you squeezed to win the ball once. In 2026 the win rate across the 4 quartiles of this metric ran from 59.6% at the top to 9.6% at the bottom. Where the same force is spent has replaced how much of it is spent.

2. Block height is not a differentiator

Where the block is set has not moved 1cm in 4 years, and between the two champions the gap is only 2.3m. Arguing about how many metres up to set the block is reopening a question that already has an answer. What has to be designed is the output that comes out of that block — how many times the line was broken after a recovery, and whether that line break carried through to the final third.

3. A star's walking is not laziness, it is a budget

In the 2022 final Messi walked 7,128m of his 12,109m. If the attack is going to be funnelled through one player, his defensive load has to be adjusted with it — that was the fork between the 4 semi-finalists. But this is an observation, not a prescription — this data cannot rule out the possibility that these were strong sides because they could afford to spare the star, and more sides made the same choice and still went out in the group stage.

What is left of my philosophy

The scoring in Chapter 9 reduces to one line. One supported, one conditional, one disconfirmed, one unresolved.

Not deleting the disconfirmed item is a condition of this report. That formation variation is not a condition for winning is a result that differs from what I thought, and it stays as it is. Variation is a cheap way of keeping the opponent from knowing what to prepare for the next match; it was not itself the reason for winning.

The one thing this report cannot answer

PMSR is match-total data. So none of the conclusions in this report has a time axis. How many seconds apart the line breaks came, how many seconds it took from breaking the defensive line to the final third, how many low-threat repetitions stacked up before a high-threat moment arrived — none of it can be seen in this data.

So the next piece of work is not tournament data but event-level logs. The two things to measure are already fixed — (a) the standard deviation of the interval between line breaks, (b) the time from breaking the defensive line to entry into the final third. If this report got as far as "what was done and how much", the next report starts from "when it was done".

The last sentence

Where to set the block did not move 1cm in 4 years.
What changed was what came out of that block,
and the sides that lost tried to make up the difference with volume of pressing.

In 2022 that error showed up as distance covered; in 2026 it showed up as pressures. FIFA changed the ruler, but football changed what was being measured.
