Signature Move series · Part 1 · The mechanics of press escape

What Comes Out Under Pressure

A turn rehearsed a hundred times in training doesn't appear in the match. It isn't merely missing — the more hurried the player is, the less it appears. The standard diagnosis on the training ground is either nerves or not enough repetition, and both end in the same prescription: do more. I have long suspected a different answer — that the player's body judged the coached movement too expensive and, in the rushed moment, chose something cheaper.

Starting pointOne scene from the training ground

It worked in training

I have watched the same scene many times. A turn drilled a hundred times in training doesn't show up in the match. It isn't simply absent — the more hurried the player, the less it appears. With time on the ball he does what he was taught; the moment an opponent is on his back, a completely different movement comes out. And usually that other movement is one nobody ever taught him — something he has been doing since he was a child.

The training ground has two standard diagnoses. He couldn't handle the pressure, or he hasn't repeated it enough. They sound like different explanations, but they end in the same place: do more.

Something about that doesn't add up. If the problem were too little repetition, the movement should fail when he has time too. But with time, it appears. If it disappears selectively, only under pressure, that isn't a problem of mastery. It's a problem of selection.

What if his body judged that movement too expensive — and, in the rushed moment, picked a cheaper one?

This piece is a record of testing that suspicion against the mechanics. The short version: the suspicion largely holds up, but I had to drop one argument I had originally intended to make. What I dropped is written down below, too.

PremiseDefining pressure precisely

Pressure is a question of time

First, what pressure actually is. Narizuka and colleagues (2026) analysed all 306 matches of the 2023 J1 League with synchronised event and tracking data, and defined pressure as the minimum arrival time: how long the fastest opponent needs to reach the player on the ball.

The result was simple. The shorter that time, the higher the probability of losing the ball.

So escaping a press is a question of what you can do inside a budget. And when the budget shrinks, things drop out in a particular order.

How it is measured — Arrival time is computed with the Fujimura–Sugihara physics-based motion model. But maximum speed and acceleration are fixed at the same values for every player (10.0 m/s, 1.0 s−1), and body orientation, passing lanes and team coordination are left out. The authors themselves say it should be read as a proxy. It is an assumption that sits awkwardly with a piece about individual differences.
Mechanism 1The first currency for buying the opponent's time

The body sends two signals at once

The individual answer to that budget is what I have called a Signature Move. Not a named dribbling trick, but the sum of a player's own touch texture, turning direction, rhythm and pressure response.

Mechanically, a Signature Move buys the opponent's time in two ways. The first is perception — the defender reads the wrong signal and goes the wrong way.

Brault and colleagues (2012) broke down the rugby side-step with motion capture and virtual reality and showed that the body sends two kinds of signal at the same time.

Deceptive signals

Honest signals

They have to actually load toward the destination, so they can't be enlarged. Nobody can be fooled here.

That is where defenders separate. Experts read the centre of mass; novices read the shoulders. And what that difference produces is counter-intuitive.

Mechanism 2The second currency

Reversing has a price

The second is mechanics — the time it takes the defender to reverse momentum he has already committed. This one does not disappear no matter how accurately he reads the move.

Van den Tillaar and colleagues (2020) measured the price list: 23 footballers, a four-metre approach.

This is the mechanical residue: the advantage that remains even when the opponent has read your move perfectly.
TwistThe term nobody counted

But I spend time too

That is the defender's side of the ledger, and one thing is missing from it. The attacker changes direction too. The price list above applies whoever turns. The attacker also has to kill his own momentum, plant a foot and rebuild speed in a new direction.

Yet the coaching conversation is always about the defender. He bit, he was wrong-footed, he lost a step. Nobody counts what the attacker paid for the move.

I have decided to call it the execution cost: the time an escape move costs the player performing it.

There are really only three reasons the attacker has the edge, and the third is the subject of this piece.

  • Foreknowledge — he knows when and where he will turn, so his perception and reaction terms are zero.
  • Pre-loading — he can set his centre of mass, plant foot and speed in advance, which shortens the braking phase.
  • Embodiment — the movement is ingrained. Executing it is cheap. That is the Signature Move, and its price is the execution cost.
CoreThe one thing the model says

The break-even point drops

The two gains behave completely differently as opponents get better. The perceptual one goes to zero; the mechanical one stays. So the line that answers how much execution cost you can afford before the move loses drops wholesale.

In short — the higher the level, the more beating a press stops being about how well you deceive and starts being about how cheap the movement is for you.

So why not just get faster?

Mechanism 3What “comfortable to the body” really is

What makes a movement cheap

So what sets the execution cost? I have long said that every player has movements that feel comfortable to his own body. It turns out that isn't a hunch. It has a proper name.

Nigg and colleagues (2017) filmed 35 runners with eight cameras at 240 Hz across three shoe types and barefoot, and showed that each runner has a preferred movement path. The definition matters: the joint trajectory that demands the least energy.

Selinger and colleagues (2015) went further. Using an exoskeleton, they artificially moved where the energetic optimum sits.

“Comfortable to the body” is not a figure of speech. It is an energetic minimum; the nervous system finds it without being told and defends it against interference.

And this is the mechanism behind “you can teach it, but he goes back to his own way.” If a movement pushed in from outside is not the player's own minimum, the body keeps trying to leave it.

One more point: even within a single movement, the room for individual difference is divided. When Deghaies and colleagues (2025) split a professional tennis serve into phases, between-player variation was about 42% in the loading phase and 15–21% in the acceleration phase. Put that together with the deceptive and honest signals above and the answer follows — a Signature Move lives only in the degrees of freedom that mechanics leaves free. So the budget for having a signature at all is allocated by the body, not by creativity.

PayoffBack to the opening scene

The borrowed move fails twice

Now the opening scene can be explained. A movement installed from outside is not the player's own minimum, so its execution cost is high from the start. That is the first loss. The second is worse.

Masters (1992) showed that skills acquired through explicit instruction break down under pressure, because the performer reinvests conscious control into them. Two later studies produced conflicting results; Mullen, Hardy and Oldham (2007) re-tested it with 32 participants and replicated Masters.

In other words, the movement a coach installs becomes more expensive exactly when the player is rushed — and that is precisely when the arrival time is short and the escape is needed.

The technique fails hardest at the exact moment it is needed.

There's a saying on the training ground that under pressure, you fall back on habit. I think this is what it really means. What comes up is not simply what was repeated most. It is what the body judged cheapest.

ConclusionThe line in player development

So where does coaching stop?

This is where the piece is easy to misread — as “so don't teach fundamentals.” That is not my argument, and I have written the opposite: dribbling, touch and basic movement need standardised teaching. They are the common language everyone has to share.

The actual argument is where the line is. Between a coaching instruction and the movement that comes out, there are four layers.

?

On the training ground, it reduces to one question

Am I correcting “where the ball has to go,” or “how he gets it there”?

If it's the second, the correction is making the movement more expensive for him — and he will pay for it at the exact moment a press arrives.

⚠ The line itself isn't perfectly firm — an open body shape (L2) is ultimately produced by a joint path (L4). Whether a conceptually clean distinction stays clean on the training ground is not yet known.

And there is a reason this line matters especially in youth development: lowering the execution cost has a window. Changing a deeply ingrained habit through relearning in adulthood is close to impossible. If L4 is being standardised now, the player will have no way to get it back later.

Caveat 1An argument that didn't survive checking

The argument I chose not to make

I originally meant to add one more line here: “So instead of uniform repetition, coaches should practise with varied variations.” I chose not to. The literature doesn't support it.

But — look closely and all three compare practice methods for reaching the same target movement: blocked or random, variation or repetition. My argument is not about the practice schedule. It is that the target movement itself should differ from player to player. No study has tested that question head on.

The test that's needed looks like this

Give two groups the same outcome constraint — get the ball through a gate behind the defender. One group is told how to do it, with demonstration and correction; the other isn't. Then compare the difference in success rate between pressured and unpressured conditions. What reinvestment theory predicts is not the absolute success rate but that difference. Run it for at least 8–12 weeks — the football study that found nothing lasted four.

Caveat 2The weakest link in the argument

Comfortable is not the same as effective

There is a hole in this, and I'd rather name it than let someone else find it.

What the research establishes is an energetic minimum; what beating a defender requires is an advantage over another person. Nothing guarantees those coincide, and the opposite is easy to imagine — a player's most comfortable turn may also be his most predictable one.

A partial answer, and why it isn't a full one — at the top level the perceptual gain goes to zero anyway, so the price of predictability has already been paid, and lowering the execution cost is the only improvement left. But that is my inference, not a tested result.

And the execution cost itself has never been measured. Of the three terms in the equation, it is the only one I can't even quote a range for. Measuring it needs three-dimensional tracking of on-ball situations, which my data can't provide.

I'd rather publish the equation with the unmeasured term in it than quietly drop the term. Dropping it makes the equation tidier — and the tidy equation is wrong at the top level.

ClosingBack to the pitch

The one move that comes out cheapest

To sum up: escaping a press is a time-budget problem. A Signature Move buys two kinds of time, and as opponents get better, the time bought by perception disappears and only the time bought by mechanics remains. What remains is the same order of magnitude as what I pay.

So the higher you go, what matters is not how spectacular the move is but how cheaply it comes out of that player's body — and that price is set by the player's own physical structure. It can't be set from outside.

Not every player can be a crack. But anyone can own one escape movement that comes out of his own body at the lowest cost, and that is the individual answer to surviving in pressing football.

The way to build it isn't more repetition. It's teaching precisely where the ball has to go, and giving the player time to find how he gets it there — not correcting him to “the right technique” the moment he fails, but leaving him in the middle ground between success and failure.

Where the ball has to go can be taught. How he gets it there has to be grown.
