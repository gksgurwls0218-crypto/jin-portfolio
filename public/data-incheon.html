They Give Away the Least, and Concede More

No side in the league allows fewer open-play chances than Incheon. Rate their matches on expected goals and only Seoul are better. Yet they sit seventh, and have conceded more than six goals above expectation. To separate luck from structure, I put all 168 league matches on the same scale and checked one step at a time.

BASELINE168 league matches · expected vs table

Second on performance, seventh in the table

Incheon have 10 wins, 8 draws and 11 defeats from 29 matches — 38 points, seventh in the table of 13 September. Re-rate every match by the expected-goal value of each shot, though, and the story changes.

Across all 168 league matches, Incheon’s expected goal difference per match is +0.39, second only to Seoul. Their actual goal difference is +0.14. Without penalties it is +0.25, 3rd in the league.

Replay every shot at its xG probability and simulate each match, and Incheon’s expected points come to 43.8. They have 38 — 5.8 points short.

It looks like a lot. Set against the rest of the league, it is not.

The missing points on their own can be put down to luck. But open up where they went, and one pattern is hard to wave away as luck.

Opponents rarely get deep into Incheon’s half, rarely produce a good shot when they do, and rarely hurt them straight after Incheon lose the ball. On suppression alone, this is the best defence in the league. So where did the extra goals come from?

TESTBreaking down the excess

Yet they conceded more than six goals above expectation

Excluding penalties and own goals, Incheon allowed 260 shots. Using every shot in the 168 matches, I worked out how many of them should have gone in if they had been stopped at the league rate.

A shot becomes a goal in three steps — where and what kind of shot was allowed, whether it reached the target (unblocked), and whether a shot on target went in.

In total, +6.9 goals — second only to Gwangju. Fewer shots were blocked than distance would predict (43 against 49.3), and more reached the target (108 against 96.1).

I then controlled for one more thing: whether a defender got to the shooter. Unchallenged shots go in more often against anyone, so the question is whether Incheon’s excess survives once that is in the model.

The chance of an excess this large by luck alone is about 8% (20,000 simulations with the adjusted model, P = 0.083). Too high to call it structure, too low to call it luck. So I looked at which shots the excess sits in.

CORE147 unchallenged shots

The ones that went in were shots nobody got to

Split the shots allowed by whether an Incheon defender acted in the three seconds before, and the excess is all on one side.

They did not allow more of them. Their share of unchallenged shots is close to the league’s. Those shots simply went in far more often than against other teams.
A correction to my own reading. In the first pass I wrote that Incheon concede more because they allow the highest share of unchallenged shots. Re-testing showed that ranking moves with the definition — 2nd with a 2-second window, 1st with 3 seconds, 3rd with 5. The gap to the league average is only 2–3 points, and the goals that share difference cost is +0.5. The problem is not how many unchallenged shots they allow, but how often those shots went in.

When ahead, Incheon allow less open-play xG than anyone in the league. They do not sit back to protect it — their own open-play xG while leading stays at 0.99 per 90, 6th in the league. Yet in this state they concede 8 against an expected 5.6, the widest overshoot of the three.

Of the 16 matches in which they took the lead, 7 saw it wiped out. Only once did they go back in front.

ATTACKPenalties · non-penalty

Take out the penalties and the attack is ninth

The other half of that second-best expected goal difference is the attack. But its xG carries the largest penalty share in the league.

Incheon have won 8 penalties and drawn 8 fouls inside the box — both league highs. Penalties are 17% of their xG; 7 of their 36 goals (19%) are penalties.

DIAGNOSISFinal third

All the way there, and no shot

Incheon reach the final third at the league’s 5th-highest rate (42.3 times per 90). But the share of those attacks that end in a shot is the lowest in the league.

That does not make them poor at creating chances. xG per final-third chain is 0.022, almost the league average (0.023). They shoot less often mainly because they rarely shoot from outside the box (3.8 per match, 11th). It is shot selection, not a shortage of chances. The real shortfalls are elsewhere — attacking straight after winning the ball (shot within 15 s: 4.4%, 10th) and set pieces without penalties (xG 0.070 per 90, 10th).

PLAYERSMugosa on · off

Without Mugosa the chances stay the same. The finishing changes

Mugosa has 12 of Incheon’s 36 goals: 5 from 6 penalties and 7 from open play and set pieces (non-penalty xG 6.3). To see how much the team leans on him, I split their minutes into time with him on the pitch and without.

What Incheon rely on him for is finishing, not creating. Without him a similar volume of chances still comes; what changes is how many go in.

DISCIPLINEHypothesis log

What did not survive testing

Five hypotheses and readings from this analysis did not survive the data. All five were mine. They stay in — a rejected hypothesis marks the edges of the conclusions that did survive.

✕

Incheon concede more because they allow the most unchallenged shots

Corrected. Their rank for the share of unchallenged shots moves with the window (2, 3 or 5 seconds before the shot): 2nd, 1st, 3rd. The gap to the league average is 2–3 points, and the goals that difference cost is +0.5. The core of the excess is not the volume but how often unchallenged shots went in.

✕

Being 5.8 points short is a structural flaw

Rejected. In the shot-level simulation, the chance of falling this short or further is 0.19. Pohang, Gimcheon and Daejeon are in the same range. The only trace of structure is the shape on the conceding side (unchallenged shots).

✕

Incheon are strong at set pieces

Rejected. My first scan read them as “1st for set-piece goals per 90”, but that included 7 penalty goals. Without penalties their set-piece xG is 0.070 per 90, 10th in the league. What is strong is winning penalties (8 fouls drawn in the box, 1st).

✕

Incheon took 18 penalty shots

Corrected. In the first scan I estimated penalties from the shot’s start coordinates (near the spot) and counted 18 — mixing in open-play shots from the same area. Counted by the event’s penalty tag, it is 8. Event type should not be inferred from coordinates.

✕

Mugosa creates Incheon’s chances

Rejected. Non-penalty xG per 90 is 0.98 with him and 0.91 without. Chance creation barely moves; goals do (1.14 vs 0.72).

Three traps in the data. ① The portal has no events for matches on 5 September. Incheon’s R27 (away at Seoul) and Bucheon–Daejeon are both empty, and the portal’s official 45-metric table leaves them out too. ② Finding untagged goals (RCV) by timestamp also catches the opposing goalkeeper’s “goal conceded” row in the same second. Only the shot row should count — otherwise the opponent gains a phantom goal. ③ An Incheon player’s own goal is logged as an Incheon shot (goal). The official table counts it in shots but not in shots on target; this piece removes it from shots.
PRESCRIPTIONPriorities

What to look at first, and what is better left alone

The diagnosis has narrowed to “they allow little, but of what they allow, unchallenged shots go in more often”. The order below is lowest cost first.

1

Straight after losing the ball, get to the shooter first

51.9% of the shots allowed came within 15 seconds of the opponent winning the ball (2nd in the league), and 18 of them went in (expected 12.0). The volume is the league’s lowest — the problem is that on those few shots, nobody got there.

On video: once the counter-press is beaten, who is first to turn back, and how far is he from the shooter?

Evidence · 147 unchallenged shots → 22 conceded (expected 15.2) · 113 challenged shots → 7 (expected 7.4)
2

Classify the shots on target on video

The excess at the “shot on target goes in” step is +4.6 goals, mostly in Kim Dong-heon’s matches. With no placement or speed in this data, the goalkeeper’s share and the shooter’s share cannot be separated.

Split the 29 goals by placement (corner / central), screening and reaction distance, and it becomes clear whether this is goalkeeper coaching or defensive positioning.

Evidence · Kim Dong-heon, 23 matches: 87 shots on target → 25 conceded (expected 20.6)
3

In the final third, play one more ball into the box

18.4% of final-third attacks end in a shot, the league’s lowest, and it is lowest when trailing too. xG per chain is average, so this is not a call for more long shots.

The share of final-third passes played into the box is 27.5%, 11th in the league. That is where to add.

Evidence · arrival → shot 18.4% (12th) · arrivals 42.3/90 (5th)
4

Rework the attack straight after winning the ball

The share of regains that become a shot within 15 seconds is 4.4%, 10th in the league; the xG from them is 9th. The defence wins the ball as well as anyone, and the moment does not turn into attack.

Evidence · shot within 15 s of a regain 4.4% (10th) · transition xG/90 0.46

Better left alone

✕

The suppression structure

Open-play xG allowed, xG allowed per final-third chain and xG allowed after losing the ball are all the league’s lowest. Dropping the line because of the excess would give up this strength first.

✕

Attacking while ahead

While leading they allow the least xG in the league and keep their own attacking xG at the 6th-highest level. The wiped-out leads did not come from refusing to sit back.

✕

The way they win penalties

8 fouls drawn in the box, the most in the league. 17% of their xG comes from here. No reason to change it.
