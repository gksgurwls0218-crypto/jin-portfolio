<Context heading="A formation is not an attacking structure">

The team sheet said 4-3-3 and 4-2-3-1; neither described what happened. The gap between each player's starting position and where he actually received the ball is where the real roles lived — a formation label is a starting point, not a conclusion.

</Context>

<Context heading="The build-up core is rest-defence — the first structure of counter-blocking">

PSG built from a Mendes–Pacho–Marquinhos back three with João Neves as the anchor — a 3-1 core. Bayern mirrored it with Upamecano–Tah–Davies and a Kimmich–Pavlović double pivot. This is what rest-defence actually means in practice: the same shape that starts the attack is also the shape left behind to screen the counter once the ball is lost. Build-up core and rest-defence core are the same four or five players doing two jobs at once — not a passing device with a defensive job attached, but one structure serving both purposes simultaneously. That double duty is exactly what gets exposed below: when PSG's platform stayed locked and Bayern's did not, the counter-transition math swung hard in one direction.

</Context>

<Context heading="Rest-attack — PSG's structure for seizing control of the game">

Both sides had a rest-attack shape on the sheet, but PSG's is the one that actually decided the match. Rest-attack runs on three fixed roles: the **Wide Runner** stretches the touchline and drags the opponent's line across; the **Binder** holds a fixed pinning position between the lines; the **Connector** links the ball-winning zone to the forwards. PSG kept Hakimi and Mendes locked as Wide Runners and Kvaratskhelia and Dembélé locked as Binders, so the Connector — Vitinha, Zaïre-Emery, João Neves rotating through — never had to search for an outlet; the release into the counter was always already there. That fixed platform is what turned 2-2 into 5-2 in twenty minutes: Kvaratskhelia and Dembélé's braces weren't four isolated moments of quality, they were the same two-touch release repeating off the same three-role scaffold, arriving faster than Bayern's back three could reset into its own rest-defence. This is the tactic that seized the game — not a formation, not one player, but PSG refusing to let their Wide Runner and Binder drift out of position even while the score was level.

</Context>

<Context heading="Match description">

In a record-breaking nine-goal first leg, PSG were 5-2 up early in the second half — Kvaratskhelia (24', 56') and Dembélé (45+5' pen, 58') scoring twice each off the wing-cut-in and one-two combinations that won last season's final, with João Neves (33') nodding in the third — after Kane's 17' penalty and Olise's 41' had twice pulled Bayern level. Then Bayern's Upamecano (65') and a superb Luis Díaz strike (68') hauled it back to 5-4. Bayern generated the higher xG (2.5 to 1.9) from 22 shots at roughly 40% conversion, but PSG's finishing ran clear of their xG: individual quality, an in-position mutation, breaking the defensive structure.

</Context>

<Context heading="Kane's paradox — build-up fluidity without an end product">

Bayern's most productive version of Kane is also the one that plays least: a striker's threat lives in the box, and every minute he spends outside it trades a small build-up gain for a large finishing loss. As the scoreline swung — 2-2, then 2-5 — Kane dropped deeper more often to help Bayern circulate under pressure, and on the surface it worked: more touches, more combinations, a team that looked less stuck. But none of it produced a second goal from him; his only goal was the 17' penalty, won and taken from the spot, not created by his own movement. In xT terms this is the tell — dropping deep adds circulation value in zones that were never short of it, while the zone that actually needed him, the box, sat empty exactly when Olise, Upamecano and Díaz needed a focal point to finish around. No spike where the spike matters. And the cost went past his own end product: every time Kane vacated the box to help build, he also vacated Bayern's own rest-defence reference point up front — the target Bayern's back three would otherwise play into to hold the ball high and slow PSG down. Without him there, those advanced possessions broke down faster and higher up the pitch, with no out-ball — and it was exactly that kind of breakdown that handed PSG's Wide Runner / Binder / Connector platform its cleanest transitions. Kane's positioning miss wasn't only his own missed chances; it was part of the mechanism that fed PSG's rest-attack goals.

</Context>

<Context heading="In the framework — and the caveat">

Rest-attack, in this match, is PSG's word. PSG skipped Phase 1 — the repeated conditioning — and went straight to Shock, exploiting the fact that a different-league Bayern had not been conditioned to their specific Wide Runner / Binder / Connector pattern; the season-scale Lure was already banked, and PSG never let the platform drift even while trailing. Bayern's four goals, by contrast, did not come from an equivalent structure repeating — a penalty, an individual strike, a poacher's finish and a wondergoal are four different mechanisms, not one repeatable pattern, and that tracks with Kane's paradox: without a fixed focal point in the box, Bayern had combinations but no repeatable release. Nine goals is still variation overload for both sides, though. Neither team ran a real defensive phase, and PSG's own shape without the ball is unstable too — when their press was beaten there was no structure behind it. An attacking philosophy needs a symmetric defensive base, and here that balance broke on both ends of the pitch, for different reasons: PSG's rest-defence structurally undercooked, Bayern's undercut by their focal forward's positioning. (Caveat: Kompany was absent, so this is a limited sample of Bayern's system.)

</Context>
